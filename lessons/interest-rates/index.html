Unit 1 · Lesson 4 Interest rates and central banks

Who sets the price of money?

The story so far You're about to graduate. You want an $8,000 loan for a used car to get to work, and the bank quotes you 9%. A year ago it was 5%. Meanwhile your savings account suddenly pays 4%. Nothing about you changed. Twelve people in Washington voted.

What

Understand interest as the price of borrowing money, and who moves it.

Why

One rate decision changes what your car, house, credit card and savings cost or earn.

How

The Federal Reserve raises rates to cool inflation and cuts them to protect jobs.

Who

Fed economists make the call. Bond traders and bank treasury analysts bet on it.

    04 Break it down your style

    The logic, step by step.

    Interest is a price

    Interest = principal × rate × time

    Borrowers are buyers of money, savers are sellers. Like any price, interest rises when demand for loans is high or the supply of savings is low. The Fed leans on it from the outside.

    How a rate hike travels

    1. The Fed raises its target for the fed funds rate, say from 4% to 4.5%. It does this mainly by raising the interest it pays banks on their reserves, so no bank lends overnight for less.
    2. Banks reprice. The prime rate, usually fed funds + 3, goes to 7.5%. Credit cards and car loans follow within weeks.
    3. Borrowing gets expensive. On an $8,000, 5-year car loan, 8% costs $162 a month; 10% costs $170. Multiply that across millions of buyers and fewer cars get bought.
    4. Saving gets rewarded. Higher savings rates make people wait to spend. Businesses delay new factories because their loans cost more too.
    5. Spending cools, so prices cool. Less demand means sellers can't raise prices as easily. This takes about 1 to 2 years to fully show up, which is why the Fed has to act before it sees the result.

    Real interest rate

    Real rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation

    What matters for spending is the real rate. A 5% rate with 8% inflation is still cheap money (−3% real). That's why in 1979 the Fed had to push rates near 20% to finally get ahead of inflation.

    Go deeper: the Fed's tools, the dual mandate and the Taylor rule

    Who decides. The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC): the 7 Fed governors plus 5 of the 12 regional Fed bank presidents vote, eight scheduled meetings a year. Congress gave the Fed a dual mandate: maximum employment and stable prices, which the Fed defines as 2% inflation over time.

    The toolkit. Interest on reserve balances (the main lever today), overnight reverse repos (a floor under rates), open market operations (buying or selling Treasury bonds), and the discount rate (emergency lending to banks). When rates hit zero, the Fed can do quantitative easing: buy long-term bonds to push long rates down. Shrinking that pile later is quantitative tightening.

    The Taylor rule, a famous rule of thumb from economist John Taylor (1993): rate ≈ 2 + inflation + 0.5 × (inflation − 2) + 0.5 × (output gap). With 4% inflation and output at normal, it suggests 2 + 4 + 1 + 0 = 7%. The Fed doesn't follow it mechanically, but traders use it to judge whether policy is tight or loose. You'll meet it again in Unit 14.

    05 Nature link your style

    The Fed is a beaver with a dam.

    A beaver can't make it rain, but it controls how fast the stream flows. Build the dam higher and water slows and pools. Open a gap and it rushes downstream. The Fed doesn't create spending either. It controls how fast money flows through the economy.

    Build it too high and the stream dries up downstream: that's a recession. Leave it too low during a flood and everything washes out: that's inflation. Beavers adjust their dams constantly as seasons change. So does the Fed, eight times a year.

    06 Notes your style

    The page you'd copy down.

    • Interest rate: the price of borrowing money, as a % of the loan per year.
    • The Fed: the US central bank. The FOMC sets a target for the federal funds rate.
    • Dual mandate: maximum employment and stable prices (2% inflation).
    • Raise rates → borrowing costs more, saving pays more → spending slows → inflation cools, unemployment can rise.
    • Cut rates → borrowing is cheap → spending and hiring pick up → risk of inflation.
    • Lags: rate changes take about 1–2 years to fully hit the economy.
    • Prime rate ≈ fed funds + 3%. Card, car and home rates are priced off it or off bond yields.
    • Real rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation.

    07 Together your style

    Grab a friend or a study group.

    Mock FOMC meeting

    Give everyone a role: a worried factory owner, a retiree living on savings, a first-time homebuyer, a Fed economist. Inflation is 4% and unemployment is 4.5%. Debate for five minutes, then vote: raise, hold or cut. Who won, and who paid for it?

    Read the real statement

    Pull up the most recent FOMC statement on federalreserve.gov. Each person highlights one sentence and translates it into plain English for the group. What did they decide, and what are they worried about?

    Chair showdown

    Both play Rate Dial in the same year. Compare your rate paths side by side. Did one of you move early and gently while the other waited and slammed the brakes? Whose economy came out better?

    09 Check

    Exam-style questions.

    Written the way a college exam would ask them. Each right answer on the first try is worth 10 xp.

    On the job: you're the bank treasury analyst

    Your bank has $500 million in savings deposits paying 1% and $400 million in car loans at fixed rates averaging 6%. Analysts expect the Fed to raise rates by 2 percentage points this year, and savers will demand about 2.5% to stay.

    Your task: estimate how the bank's yearly interest profit changes. Why are fixed-rate loans a risk when rates rise?

    See a model answer

    Before: the bank earns 6% × $400M = $24M and pays 1% × $500M = $5M, so $19M of interest profit.

    After: the loans are fixed, so they still earn $24M. Deposit costs rise to 2.5% × $500M = $12.5M. Profit falls to $11.5M, down $7.5M (about 39%).

    Why it's risky: the bank's costs float with the Fed but its income is locked in. This "interest rate risk" is exactly what sank Silicon Valley Bank in 2023. Treasury teams manage it by matching fixed and floating assets and liabilities, or by hedging.
