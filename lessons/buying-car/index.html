Unit 7 · Lesson 2 Cars

The payment is $239. The car costs $559 a month.

The story so far In Lesson 4.3 you picked a used car for $12,000: $2,000 down, and a 4-year loan at 7% for the other $10,000. That's $239.46 a month. Now it's signing day. The dealer adds sales tax, title and registration, and a paperwork fee: $1,070 more, due today. Then the car needs insurance ($150 a month), gas ($120) and upkeep like oil changes and tires ($50). The real cost of having this car is about $559 a month. This lesson shows how to see that number before you sign, and how not to overpay.

What

Buying a car means paying the price, the taxes and fees, and then the monthly cost to keep it running.

Why

For most people a car is the second-biggest bill after rent, and it loses value every year you own it.

How

Pick a car that fits the 20/4/10 rule, get a loan offer before you shop, agree on the out-the-door price, and get a used car inspected.

Who

Auto loan officers at banks and credit unions approve loans. Car salespeople and finance managers at dealers sell the car and the loan. Auto analysts track prices and resale values.

What you're looking at: your car and what it costs each month. The loan payment is the orange block on top. Insurance, gas and upkeep stack under it: $239 + $150 + $120 + $50 = about $559.

01 See it

Buying a car, with your eyes open.

Car buying has four money traps: paying for value that disappears, surprise fees, forgetting the costs after you drive away, and getting talked into a long loan. Here's how to spot each one.

New or used: the value drop

A car is worth less every year. That loss is called depreciation, and it's steepest at the start: a new car often loses about 20% in its first year, and around half its value in about five years. Buy a car that's a few years old and the first owner has already paid for that big early drop.

In this example
A $26,000 new car
After 1 year at 20% off: $26,000 × 0.80 = $20,800. You lost $5,200, about $100 a week, just by owning it.

The out-the-door price

The sticker price isn't what you pay. The out-the-door price adds sales tax (it depends on your state), title and registration fees for your state, and dealer paperwork fees. Always ask for the out-the-door number in writing.

In this example
Your car
$12,000 price + $720 sales tax (6%) + $150 title and registration + $200 paperwork fee = $13,070. That's the $1,070 due at signing.

The full cost of owning it

The cost of ownership is the loan payment plus everything else the car needs: insurance (Lesson 6.1), gas, and upkeep. Older cars cost more to fix; newer cars cost more to insure. Get an insurance quote before you buy.

In this example
Each month
$239.46 payment + $150 insurance + $120 gas + $50 upkeep = $559.46.
Part of your $3,000 income
$559.46$3,000 = about 19%.

The 20/4/10 rule

A simple test for whether a car fits: the 20/4/10 rule. Put at least 20% down, borrow for no more than 4 years, and keep the payment plus insurance at or under 10% of your monthly income before taxes. It's a guideline, not a law, but missing it is a sign the car is a stretch.

In this example
20% down?
$2,000$12,000 = 17%. Close, but short: 20% would be $12,000 × 0.20 = $2,400.
4 years?
48 months. Pass.
10%?
$239.46 + $150 = $389.46, but 10% of $3,000 is $300. Over by $89.46.

Loan length

The line marks 10% of $3,000, which is $300.

What you're looking at: a car cost checker for someone earning $3,000 a month before taxes, putting $2,000 down and borrowing the rest at 7%. Slide the price and pick the loan length. The bar is the loan payment plus $150 of insurance, with a line at the 10% limit.

Shop like a pro

1. Get preapproved first. Ask your bank or credit union for a loan offer before you visit a dealer. Your credit score (Lesson 4.1) sets the rate. If the dealer can beat it, great; if not, you already have a loan. 2. Negotiate the out-the-door price, not the monthly payment. "What payment do you want?" lets a dealer stretch the loan to 6 or 7 years so the payment looks small. 3. For a used car, pay a mechanic you choose to inspect it (often $100 to $200), and check its history report with the VIN, the car's ID number.

In this example
Preapproved at 7%
$239.46 a month for 4 years: $1,494.08 of interest.
Dealer's offer at 9%
$248.85 a month for 4 years: $1,944.80 of interest. Your preapproval saves $450.72.

    04 Break it down

    The logic, step by step.

    Four numbers to work out before you sign.

    Step 1: The out-the-door price

    Price + sales tax + title and registration + dealer fees = what you really pay
    In this example
    Your car
    $12,000 + $720 + $150 + $200 = $13,070.

    Step 2: The down payment check

    Price × 0.20 = the down payment to aim for
    In this example
    $12,000 car
    $12,000 × 0.20 = $2,400. You put $2,000 down, $400 short.

    Step 3: The 10% check

    Loan payment + insurance ≤ monthly income × 0.10
    In this example
    Your car
    $239.46 + $150 = $389.46, and $3,000 × 0.10 = $300. Over.

    Step 4: The full monthly cost

    Payment + insurance + gas + upkeep = what the car really costs
    In this example
    Each month
    $239.46 + $150 + $120 + $50 = $559.46.

    Worked example: a car that fits

    1. A cheaper car. An $8,000 used car, with the same $2,000 down.
    2. 20% down? $2,000$8,000 = 25%. Pass.
    3. 4 years? Borrow $8,000 − $2,000 = $6,000 at 7% for 48 months: $143.68 a month. Pass.
    4. 10%? $143.68 + $150 insurance = $293.68, under $300. Pass.
    5. The payoff. $239.46 − $143.68 = $95.78 a month less, about $4,600 over the 4 years, that can go to your emergency fund or investing.
    05 Nature link

    The owl that moves into a used nest.

    Great horned owls usually don't build their own nests. They move into old nests that hawks, crows or squirrels built in an earlier year. The hard work is already done, and the nest still does the job.

    What you're looking at: an illustration, not data. A great horned owl in an old stick nest another bird built, with that bird flying off in the distance.

    Why this sticks. The first owner of a car pays for its steepest value drop, the way the hawk does the hard work of building. A good used car, checked by a mechanic, lets you skip that cost and still get where you're going.

    06 Notes

    The page you'd copy down.

    • Depreciation: a new car often loses about 20% the first year, around half in about 5 years. $26,000 × 0.80 = $20,800.

    • 20/4/10 rule: 20% down, loan of 4 years or less, payment + insurance ≤ 10% of income before taxes.

    • Negotiate the out-the-door price, not the monthly payment.

    09 Check

    Exam-style questions.

    Written the way a college exam would ask them. Each right answer on the first try is worth 10 xp.

    On the job: you're a credit union auto loan officer

    A member, Leo, wants your advice before buying a car. Here are his numbers:

    • He earns $3,500 a month before taxes.
    • Car A, new: $32,000. 20% down ($6,400), borrow $25,600 at 7% for 6 years: $436.45 a month. Insurance: $180 a month.
    • Car B, 3 years old: $12,000. 20% down ($2,400), borrow $9,600 at 7% for 4 years: $229.88 a month. Insurance: $120 a month.

    Your task: check both cars with the 20/4/10 rule and tell Leo which one fits.

    See a model answer

    His 10% limit: $3,500 × 0.10 = $350 for payment plus insurance.

    Car A: 20% down, yes. But 6 years is longer than 4, and $436.45 + $180 = $616.45, which is $266.45 over his limit. It fails two of the three tests. Over 6 years he'd also pay $436.45 × 72 − $25,600 = $5,824.40 of interest.

    Car B: 20% down, yes. 4 years, yes. $229.88 + $120 = $349.88, just under $350. It passes all three.

    Advice: Car B, after an inspection by his own mechanic. It costs $616.45 − $349.88 = $266.57 a month less, and the first owner already took the biggest value drop.
