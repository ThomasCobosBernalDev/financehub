Unit 16 · Lesson 3 Banks and markets

Lock in a price today. Or buy the right to choose later.

The story so far The bike chain (Lessons 16.1 and 16.2) needs 100 tons of aluminum for frames in 6 months, and aluminum prices jump around. A rise could wipe out a year's profit. Meanwhile, you own 100 shares of the chain's stock at $26, and you think it could climb, or crash. Both problems have the same kind of tool: a contract about what happens next. This lesson shows how they work, and why they can protect you or burn you.

What

A derivative is a contract whose value comes from something else, like a stock or aluminum. Futures and options are the most common.

Why

Companies use them to lock in prices and protect profits. Investors use them to protect portfolios, or to bet with less money up front.

How

A future is a promise to trade at a set price later. An option is the right, not the duty, to trade at a set price, and you pay for that right up front.

Who

Derivatives traders at banks make markets in them. Risk managers at companies use them to hedge. Commodity traders deal in oil, metals and crops.

What you're looking at: a call option on the chain's stock. For a $200 price paid today, you get the right to buy 100 shares at $30 each any time until June. If the stock stays below $30, you just don't use it. That's what "right, not duty" means.

01 See it

A contract whose value comes from something else.

Stocks and bonds are pieces of a company or a loan. A derivative is different: it's a contract about something else, called the underlying. When the underlying's price moves, the contract's value moves too.

Futures: locking in a price

A futures contract is a deal to buy or sell a set amount at a set price on a future date. Both sides must go through with it. Futures trade on exchanges like the CME in Chicago, for oil, metals, crops, stock indexes and more.

In this example
The chain's futures
It agrees today to buy 100 tons of aluminum in 6 months at $2,500 a ton: $250,000.
If aluminum rises to $3,000
The futures gain ($3,000 − $2,500) × 100 = $50,000, covering the higher price. The chain still pays about $250,000 in total.
If aluminum falls to $2,000
The futures lose ($2,500 − $2,000) × 100 = $50,000, but the metal is $50,000 cheaper. Still about $250,000.

Hedgers and speculators

The chain isn't trying to win; it wants a price it can plan around. Using a derivative to reduce a risk you already have is hedging. Using one to bet on a price, with no risk to protect, is speculating. Markets need both: speculators take the other side of hedgers' trades.

In this example
Hedger
The bike chain, which really needs the aluminum.
Speculator
A trader who sold the chain those futures, betting aluminum won't rise.

Options: the right, not the duty

An option gives its buyer a choice. A call option is the right to buy at a set price; a put option is the right to sell. The set price is the strike price, the last day is the expiration, and the price you pay for the option is the premium. One stock option contract covers 100 shares.

In this example
Your call
Strike $30, expires in June, premium $2 a share × 100 shares = $200.
Stock at $36 in June
Buy at $30, worth $36: ($36 − $30) × 100 = $600. Minus the $200 premium: $400 profit.
Stock at $28 in June
Why buy at $30? You don't. The option expires, and you lose the $200 premium, no more.

Break-even and leverage

A call starts making money only when the stock passes the strike plus the premium, its break-even. Options are a form of leverage (Lesson 13.1): a small amount controls a lot of stock, so the percent gains and losses are huge.

Call break-even = strike + premium per share
In this example
Break-even
$30 + $2 = $32.
Stock goes $26 → $36
Owning 100 shares: $2,600 becomes $3,600, $1,000$2,600 = about 38%. The call: $200 becomes $600, $400$200 = 200%.
Stock goes $26 → $28
Shares: up about 8%. The call: −100%.

What you're looking at: your profit in June at each stock price from $20 to $40. Blue is owning 100 shares bought at $26. Green is the $30 call bought for $200. The dashed line is zero profit. The dots show where you are at the price you pick.

A put as insurance

Worried your shares could crash? Buy a put. It's like insurance (Lesson 6.1): you pay a premium, and if the price falls below the strike, the put pays you back.

In this example
The put
Right to sell 100 shares at $24, premium $1 a share: $100.
Put break-even
A put gains as the price falls, so subtract: strike − premium = $24 − $1 = $23.
Stock crashes to $15
Your shares lose ($26 − $15) × 100 = $1,100. The put pays ($24 − $15) × 100 = $900. Total loss: $1,100 − $900 + $100 premium = $300, instead of $1,100.

    04 Break it down

    The logic, step by step.

    To work out any option at expiration, take four steps.

    Step 1: Call or put?

    Call: right to buy. Put: right to sell.
    In this example
    Your option
    A call, strike $30.

    Step 2: Would you use it?

    Call: only if the stock is above the strike. Put: only if it's below.
    In this example
    Stock at $36
    Yes: buying at $30 beats $36.

    Step 3: What's it worth?

    Call: (stock − strike) × 100. Put: (strike − stock) × 100. Or zero.
    In this example
    The call
    ($36 − $30) × 100 = $600.

    Step 4: Subtract the premium

    Profit = value − premium paid
    In this example
    Profit
    $600 − $200 = $400.

    Worked example: should you protect your shares?

    The chain reports earnings next month, and the stock could jump or crash. Here are the facts:

    • You own: 100 shares bought at $26.
    • A $24 put: costs $100 for 100 shares.
    • A $30 call: costs $200 for 100 shares.
    1. Name your risk. You already own the shares, so your risk is a crash. That calls for a hedge, not a bet.
    2. Price the hedge. The put costs $100, about $100$2,600 = 4% of your shares' value.
    3. Check the worst case. With the put, the most you can lose is ($26 − $24) × 100 + $100 = $300, however far the stock falls.
    4. Skip the bet. Buying the call too would add a $200 speculation on top of shares that already gain if the stock rises.
    5. The answer. If a crash would really hurt you, the $100 put is cheap insurance. If you can live with the swings, keep the $100: like all insurance, most of the time it expires unused.
    05 Nature link

    Desert seeds hedge their bets.

    In deserts, some years bring rain and some bring none. Many desert annual plants don't let all their seeds sprout at once. Some sprout the first year; others wait in the soil for years. If one year is a drought, the waiting seeds survive. Biologists call this bet-hedging.

    What you're looking at: an illustration, not data. Desert soil where some seeds from the same plant have sprouted this year, while others wait underground for a later rain.

    Why this sticks. The waiting seeds are a put option: they give up some growth this year to make sure a bad year can't wipe the plant out. A good hedge costs a little every year so that no single year can ruin you.

    The page you'd copy down.

    09 Check

    Exam-style questions.

    Written the way a college exam would ask them. Each right answer on the first try is worth 10 xp.

    On the job: you're a risk manager at an airline

    Your airline will buy 1 million gallons of jet fuel in 3 months. The CFO wants a plan. Here's the situation:

    • Fuel futures price for 3 months out: $2.50 a gallon.
    • The airline's budget: built on $2.50 a gallon, or $2.5 million.
    • Option 1: buy fuel futures at $2.50.
    • Option 2: buy call options with a $2.60 strike, for a premium of $0.10 a gallon.

    Your task: what does each choice cost if fuel ends at $3.20? And if it falls to $2.00? Which would you recommend, and why?

    See a model answer

    Futures, fuel at $3.20: fuel costs $3.2 million, the futures gain ($3.20 − $2.50) × 1 million = $700,000. Net $2.5 million. At $2.00: fuel $2.0 million, futures lose $500,000. Net still $2.5 million.

    Calls, fuel at $3.20: fuel $3.2 million, the calls pay ($3.20 − $2.60) × 1 million = $600,000, premium $100,000. Net $3.2 − $0.6 + $0.1 = $2.7 million. At $2.00: the calls expire; fuel $2.0 million + $100,000 premium = $2.1 million.

    To the CFO: futures give a fixed $2.5 million no matter what, which matches our budget exactly. Calls cost $100,000 up front and cap our cost at $2.7 million, but let us save if fuel falls. If our priority is hitting the budget, use futures. If we can afford a bit more risk for the chance of savings, calls are worth it.
