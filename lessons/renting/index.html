Unit 7 · Lesson 1 Housing

The rent is $900. Moving in costs $1,850.

The story so far You found the apartment near the bike shop: $900 a month. You earn $3,000 a month before taxes. The landlord says yes, and then hands you the move-in bill: the first month's rent, a $900 security deposit, and a $50 application fee. That's $1,850 before you've slept there one night. Then come the lights, internet and renters insurance. This lesson shows how much rent you can really afford, what you pay to move in, what a lease locks you into, and how to get your deposit back when you leave.

What

Renting means paying a landlord each month to live in their place. The lease is the contract with the rules and the price.

Why

Rent is the biggest bill most young adults have. Pick one that's too high and every other money goal gets squeezed.

How

Keep rent near 30% of your income before taxes, save the move-in cash first, read the lease, and photograph everything on move-in day.

Who

Leasing agents show units and check applications. Property managers run buildings for owners. Real estate analysts figure out what rents a building can earn.

What you're looking at: the door to apartment 2B and the bill you pay before you get its key. The rent is $900 a month, but moving in takes $900 + $900 + $50 = $1,850.

01 See it

Renting, step by step.

Finding a place is the fun part. The money part has four pieces: the rent you can afford, the cash to move in, the full monthly cost, and the lease you sign.

How much rent can you afford?

Two rules of thumb. Yours: the 30% rule says keep rent at or below 30% of your monthly income before taxes. The government counts anyone above that as "cost-burdened." The landlord's: many ask that you earn at least 3 times the rent. They're close: 3 times the rent is the same as rent being about 33% of income.

In this example
Your 30% cap
$3,000 × 0.30 = $900. The apartment is right at the limit.
Landlord's check
3 × $900 = $2,700. You earn $3,000, so you pass.

People splitting it

The line marks 30% of $3,000, which is $900.

What you're looking at: a rent checker for someone earning $3,000 a month before taxes. Slide the rent and pick how many people split it. The bar is your share of rent as a part of your income, with a line at 30%. With roommates, it assumes each person earns $3,000 too.

The cash to move in

Before you get keys, most landlords want the first month's rent, a security deposit (often one month's rent), and an application fee for the credit check. Some also want the last month up front. A low credit score, like you saw in Lesson 4.1, can mean a bigger deposit. Save this cash before you start touring, and don't touch your emergency fund for it.

In this example
Move-in bill
$900 rent + $900 deposit + $50 fee = $1,850.

The real monthly cost

Rent is only the start. Add utilities (electricity, gas, water, internet) and the renters insurance from Lesson 6.1. Ask the landlord which utilities are included and what last winter's bills were.

In this example
Each month
$900 rent + $110 utilities + $15 renters insurance = $1,025.

Reading the lease

A lease is a contract, so read it all before signing. Look for: the term (usually 12 months), the day rent is due and the grace period before a late fee, the late fee, what it costs to break the lease early, who fixes what, and the rules on pets and guests. If your income is too low for the 3 times rule, some landlords take a guarantor: someone who promises to pay if you don't.

In this example
Your lease says
12 months. Rent due on the 1st, 5-day grace period, then a $50 late fee. Breaking it early costs 2 months' rent: 2 × $900 = $1,800.

Roommates

Splitting a bigger place is the fastest way to cut rent. But on most shared leases, each roommate has joint liability: if one roommate leaves, the landlord can ask you for the whole rent. Write a roommate agreement: who pays what, by when, and what happens if someone moves out.

In this example
A 2-bedroom
$1,400 rent: $1,4002 people = $700 each. That's $900 − $700 = $200 less a month, or $200 × 12 = $2,400 a year.
If your roommate leaves
The landlord can still ask you for all $1,400.

Getting your deposit back

On move-in day, walk through with the landlord and take dated photos of every scratch and stain. When you leave, the landlord can only take money for real damage, not normal wear and tear like faded paint or worn carpet. State law sets a deadline to return your deposit, often 14 to 30 days, with a written list of anything they kept.

In this example
Move-out
Worn carpet: $0, that's normal wear. A hole in the wall: $120 to fix. You get $900 − $120 = $780 back.

    The logic, step by step.

    Four checks before you sign anything.

    Step 1: Your rent cap

    Monthly income before taxes × 0.30 = most rent you should pay
    In this example
    You
    $3,000 × 0.30 = $900.

    Step 2: The landlord's check

    3 × rent ≤ your monthly income
    In this example
    $900 apartment
    3 × $900 = $2,700, which is less than $3,000. You pass.

    Step 3: Move-in cash

    First month + security deposit + fees = cash to move in
    In this example
    Apartment 2B
    $900 + $900 + $50 = $1,850.

    Step 4: The full monthly cost

    Rent + utilities + renters insurance = what housing really costs
    In this example
    Each month

    Worked example: alone or with a roommate?

    1. Alone. $900 rent + $110 utilities + $15 insurance = $1,025 a month.
    2. With a roommate. A 2-bedroom is $1,400 with $140 of utilities. Your half: $1,400 + $1402 = $770, plus your own $15 insurance = $785.
    3. The difference. $1,025 − $785 = $240 a month, or $240 × 12 = $2,880 a year.
    4. The catch. Joint liability: if your roommate leaves, you could owe the full $1,400.
    5. The call. Roommate, if it's someone you trust and you both sign a roommate agreement.
    05 Nature link

    The hermit crab rents its home.

    A hermit crab doesn't grow its own shell. It moves into an empty one left by a sea snail, lives there while it fits, and moves out when it outgrows it. It picks a shell that fits its body now, not the biggest one on the beach.

    What you're looking at: an illustration, not data. A hermit crab that has outgrown its small shell, heading to a bigger empty one.

    Why this sticks. A shell that's too big is heavy to carry and hard to defend. Rent that's too big does the same to your budget. Pick the place that fits your income now, leave it the way you found it, and move up when you grow.

    06 Notes

    The page you'd copy down.

    • 30% rule: rent ≤ income before taxes × 0.30. $3,000 × 0.30 = $900.
    • Landlord's 3 times rule: income ≥ 3 × rent. 3 × $900 = $2,700.
    • Move-in cash = first month + security deposit + fees: $900 + $900 + $50 = $1,850.
    • Real monthly cost = rent + utilities + renters insurance: $900 + $110 + $15 = $1,025.
    • Lease: check the term, due date, grace period, late fee, early-break cost, repairs, pets. A guarantor can help if your income is short.
    • Roommates cut rent ($1,400 split 2 ways = $700) but joint liability means you can owe the whole rent.
    • Deposit: photos on move-in; landlords can charge for damage, not normal wear and tear; state law sets the return deadline.

    09 Check

    Exam-style questions.

    Written the way a college exam would ask them. Each right answer on the first try is worth 10 xp.

    On the job: you're the leasing agent

    Jasmine wants to rent an apartment in your building. Here are her numbers:

    • She earns $3,600 a month before taxes.
    • She wants a 1-bedroom that rents for $1,250 a month.
    • Your building's rule: income must be at least 3 times the rent.
    • You also have a 1-bedroom for $1,200, and a 2-bedroom for $1,600 that two people can share.

    Your task: does Jasmine qualify for the $1,250 apartment? If not, what are her options?

    See a model answer

    The $1,250 unit: 3 × $1,250 = $3,750. She earns $3,600, which is $150 short, so she doesn't qualify on her own.

    Option 1, the $1,200 unit: 3 × $1,200 = $3,600. She qualifies exactly, and it's $50 less a month.

    Option 2, a roommate: the 2-bedroom split two ways is $1,6002 = $800 each. That's 3 × $800 = $2,400 needed each, and she's well over. She saves $1,250 − $800 = $450 a month, but she'd have joint liability for the full $1,600.

    Option 3, a guarantor: someone with higher income signs on and promises to pay if she can't.

    Also: by the 30% rule, her cap is $3,600 × 0.30 = $1,080. Even the $1,200 unit is over it, so the roommate option is the easiest on her budget.
