Unit 5 · Lesson 6 Market drops

When the market drops, don't drop out.

The story so far You've got VOO in your Oakline account and $10 going in every Friday. Then one morning the app is red. Your fund is down 4% this week, the news says "markets tumble," and a friend texts that he sold everything. You wonder if you should too. This lesson is about that moment, because it will come, more than once.

What

A bear market is a drop of 20% or more from the market's last high. Smaller dips happen almost every year.

Why

Drops are the price of stocks' higher long-run returns. Selling in a panic turns a temporary drop into a permanent loss.

How

Invest only money you won't need for years, keep an emergency fund, keep buying on a schedule, and don't sell because prices fell.

Who

Financial advisors spend much of their job talking clients out of panic selling. Portfolio managers at funds decide how much risk to hold.

What you're looking at: a sketch shaped like the real 2020 drop, when the S&P 500 fell 34% in about a month and was back at a new high five months later. The person is holding on instead of jumping off.

01 See it

Drops are normal. Selling is optional.

Every chart below uses real S&P 500 history. Look at how often prices fall, how deep the worst falls went, and what time does to the odds.

Prices swing every year

How much and how fast prices move up and down is called volatility. Stocks are volatile: some years are great, some are awful, and you can't tell which is coming. Drag across the chart to see each year.

In this example
Volatility
From 1928 to 2025, the S&P 500 with dividends fell in 26 of 98 years, about 1 in 4. Its worst year was 1931, −43.8%. Its best was 1954, +52.6%.

What you're looking at: real data. Each bar is one year from 1928 to 2025, showing the S&P 500's total return including dividends, from Aswath Damodaran's NYU dataset. Bars above the middle line are gains, bars below are losses. The scale runs from −50% to +55%.

Corrections and bear markets

A correction is a drop of 10% or more from a recent high. A bear market is 20% or more. The opposite, a long climb, is a bull market. Getting back to the old high is the recovery. Tap a bear market to read what happened.

In this example
Correction
From February to April 2025, the S&P 500 fell about 19% as new tariffs shook markets. It was back at a new high by late June.
Bear market
From January 3 to October 12, 2022, it fell 25.4% as interest rates jumped.
Recovery
It passed its January 2022 high on January 19, 2024, about 2 years after the drop began.

Time shrinks the chance of a loss

Your time horizon is how long until you need the money. Over one year, stocks lose money often. Over long stretches, losses got rare, because good years had time to outweigh bad ones. Slide to pick how many years you hold.

In this example
Time horizon
Money for a car next year has a 1-year horizon: about 1 in 4 one-year stretches lost money. Retirement money at 20 has a 45-year horizon. Every 20-year stretch since 1928, all 79 of them, ended with a gain.

Why selling after a drop hurts

Panic selling means selling because prices fell and you're scared. It locks in the loss, and then you have to guess when to get back in. Some of the market's best days come right after its worst ones: J.P. Morgan found that over the 20 years it studied, 7 of the 10 best days came within two weeks of the 10 worst, and missing just those 10 best days cut an investor's ending value roughly in half. Part of the urge comes from loss aversion: psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky found that losses feel about twice as strong as gains of the same size.

In this example
Panic selling
$10,000 in the S&P 500 at the start of 2008 was worth $6,345 after the year's −36.6%. Sell then and hold cash, and five years later you'd have about $6,380. Stay invested, and by the end of 2013 it was about $14,350.
Loss aversion
Watching $100 disappear feels about as bad as gaining $200 feels good. That's why a red week feels like an emergency even when it isn't.

Keep buying on a schedule

Dollar-cost averaging means investing the same amount on a schedule, like $100 a month, no matter what. When prices drop, the same $100 buys more shares, so drops quietly help you. Drag the middle month's price.

In this example
Dollar-cost averaging
$100 a month at $50, then $25, then $50 buys 2 + 4 + 2 = 8 shares for $300. At $50 they're worth $400, even though the price ended where it started.

What you're looking at: a calculator with made-up prices, not market data. Each month you invest $100. The coins show how many shares $100 buys at that month's price. Month 1 and month 3 stay at $50.

The real protection: a plan made before the drop

Drops hurt most when you need the money during one. So keep money you'll need within about five years, like your emergency fund or a car down payment, in savings, not stocks. With that cushion, you never have to sell low to pay a bill.

In this example
The plan
Your $3,600 emergency fund sits in savings. Your VOO is for 10+ years from now. When the market drops 25%, nothing in your life forces a sale, so you keep your $10 a week going in.

04 Break it down

The logic, step by step.

Four calculations explain why drops feel so big and why time and steady buying help.

Step 1: The size of a drop

Subtract the old value from the new value, then divide by the old value.

Worked example: a bear market with your $10 a week

  1. Before. You hold $2,000 of VOO for retirement and $3,600 of emergency savings. You add $10 every Friday.
  2. The drop. Prices fall 25%: $1,500 − $2,000$2,000 = −25%. Your account shows $1,500.
  3. The urge. Loss aversion says sell. Your plan says nothing changed: you won't touch this money for decades, and bills come from savings.
  4. Keep buying. Each $10 now buys shares about 25% cheaper than before the drop.
  5. The recovery. To get back, prices need 0.250.75 ≈ 33.3%. In 2022's bear market, that took until January 2024, and your cheap Friday shares gained even more.
05 Nature link

Bears sleep through the winter.

A black bear doesn't hunt through a blizzard. It eats well in the fall, builds up fat, and then spends the winter in its den, living off that stored fat for months. When spring comes, it walks out into a world with food again.

What you're looking at: an illustration, not data. A bear sleeps in a snowy den while snow falls outside.

Why this sticks. In a bear market, act like a bear in winter. Your emergency fund is the fat you stored up, so you don't have to go out and sell in the storm. Wait in the den, keep your small weekly buys going, and walk out when spring comes.

  • Time horizon: when you need the money. Losing stretches since 1928: 1 year 26.5%, 5 years 11.7%, 10 years 5.6%, 20 years 0%.

Exam-style questions.

On the job: you're the financial advisor

A 30-year-old client calls. Her retirement account, all in an S&P 500 index fund, fell from $40,000 to $30,000 in a bear market. She wants to sell everything "until things calm down" and buy back later. She has a $9,000 emergency fund and adds $500 a month to the account.

Your task: work out the size of her drop and the gain needed to get back, then explain what selling now would require to work out, and what you'd suggest instead.

See a model answer

The numbers: $30,000 − $40,000$40,000 = −25%. To get back she needs 0.250.75 ≈ 33.3%, so $30,000 must grow by $10,000.

What selling requires: she'd have to be right twice, selling before more drops and buying back before the rebound. Big rebound days often come within days of the worst days, and in 2022's bear market, prices were back at the old high by January 2024. If she waits for things to feel calm, she'll likely buy back higher than she sold.

What to suggest: her money is for retirement, 35 years away, and she has $9,000 of emergency savings, so nothing forces her to sell. Keep the $500 a month going: each deposit now buys about a third more shares than before the drop. If the swings are truly unbearable, the long-term fix is a mix with some bonds chosen calmly, not a sale in a panic.
