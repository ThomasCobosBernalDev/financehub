Unit 10 · Lesson 1 Money math

Up 10%, then down 10%. You end up with less.

The story so far Business at the bike shop is booming, so the owner gives you a 10% raise: your $2,400 a month becomes $2,640. A year later, sales slow down and everyone takes a 10% cut. You figure you're back at $2,400. But your new pay is $2,376, $24 less than where you started. Nobody cheated you. That's just how percents work. This lesson shows the math behind almost every money question: tips, raises, discounts, interest and investment returns.

What

A percent means "out of 100." Growth in percent is how money questions are asked: rates, returns, raises and prices.

Why

Percents can fool you: a 50% loss needs a 100% gain to recover, and two discounts don't simply add up.

How

Turn every percent into a decimal, multiply, and always ask: percent of what?

Who

Financial analysts measure growth in sales and profits. Actuaries use percents to price risk. Pricing analysts set discounts that still make money.

What you're looking at: your monthly pay, drawn to scale. The dashed line marks your starting $2,400. The raise added 10% of $2,400, which is $240. The cut took 10% of a bigger number, $2,640, which is $264. So you end $24 below the line.

01 See it

Percent, piece by piece.

Six ideas cover nearly all the percent math you'll meet in money: finding a percent, percent change, points vs. percent, the comeback trap, stacked discounts and compounding.

A percent of a number

A percent means "out of 100." To use one, turn it into a decimal by moving the point two places left (15% = 0.15), then multiply.

In this example
Your raise
10% = 0.10. $2,400 × 0.10 = $240. New pay: $2,400 + $240 = $2,640, or in one step $2,400 × 1.10 = $2,640.
A tip
15% on a $60 meal: $60 × 0.15 = $9.

Percent change

Percent change compares the change to the starting number: new minus old, divided by old.

Percent change = new − oldold
In this example
The cut
$2,376 − $2,640$2,640 = −$264$2,640 = −0.10, a 10% drop.
Start to finish
$2,376 − $2,400$2,400 = −$24$2,400 = −0.01, 1% less overall.

Percentage points vs. percent

When a rate itself changes, there are two ways to say it. Percentage points are the plain difference. Percent change compares that difference to the old rate. News headlines mix them up all the time.

In this example
A savings rate from 4% to 5%
Up 1 percentage point (5 − 4). But that's 5 − 44 = 0.25, a 25% increase in what you earn.

The comeback trap

A loss and the gain needed to undo it are not the same size, because the gain starts from a smaller number. That's why your 10% cut didn't cancel your 10% raise, and why big investment losses hurt so much.

In this example
A stock falls 50%
$100 × 0.50 = $50. A 50% gain only gets it to $50 × 1.50 = $75. To get back to $100 it needs $100 − $50$50 = 1.00, a 100% gain.

Stacked discounts

"20% off, plus an extra 10% off" is not 30% off. The second discount comes off the already-lower price. Multiply the parts you keep.

In this example
A $100 jacket
$100 × 0.80 = $80, then $80 × 0.90 = $72. That's 28% off, not 30%.

Simple vs. compound growth

Simple growth adds the same amount every year, figured on the starting number. Compound growth figures each year's gain on the new total, so the growth itself grows. That's the engine behind savings interest (Lesson 3.3), investing (Lesson 5.1) and, in reverse, credit card debt (Lesson 4.2).

Compound value = start × (1 + rate)years
In this example
$1,000 at 10% for 3 years, simple
$1,000 + 3 × $100 = $1,300.
Same, compound
$1,000 × 1.10 × 1.10 × 1.10 = $1,331. The extra $31 is growth earned on growth.

A quick shortcut for compounding is the rule of 72: divide 72 by the yearly percent to estimate how many years it takes to double.

Years to double ≈ 72yearly growth %
In this example
Growing 8% a year
728 = 9 years to double: $1,000 becomes about $2,000.

What you're looking at: $1,000 growing at the rate and for the years you pick. Each column is one year. The gray part is what simple growth would give you; the green on top is the extra from compounding. It's a math example, not a promise of any real return.

    04 Break it down

    The logic, step by step.

    Three moves solve almost any percent problem.

    Step 1: Turn the percent into a decimal

    Move the decimal point two places left: 10% = 0.10, 7% = 0.07
    In this example
    Your raise
    10% becomes 0.10.

    Step 2: Use a multiplier

    Up by a percent: × (1 + decimal)   ·   down by a percent: × (1 − decimal)
    In this example
    Raise, then cut
    $2,400 × 1.10 × 0.90 = $2,400 × 0.99 = $2,376.

    Step 3: Ask "percent of what?"

    Percent change is always measured from the starting number
    In this example
    The cut
    10% of $2,640, not 10% of $2,400. That's why it's $264, not $240.

    Worked example: getting back to $2,400

    1. Where you are. $2,376 a month after the raise and the cut.
    2. The gap. $2,400 − $2,376 = $24.
    3. Percent of what? Of the new, lower pay: $24$2,376 = 0.0101.
    4. The answer. You'd need a raise of about 1.01%, a bit more than 1%, to get back where you started.
    5. The lesson. Losses and cuts always need a slightly bigger gain to undo, and the bigger the loss, the bigger the gap.
    05 Nature link

    Duckweed doubles.

    Duckweed, a tiny floating plant, can double the space it covers in about two days when conditions are right. That's compounding in nature: each new plant starts making more plants. A pond can look nearly empty, and then be covered within days.

    What you're looking at: an illustration, not data. If duckweed doubled every 2 days and covered a pond on day 60, it would cover half the pond on day 58, a quarter on day 56, and only an eighth on day 54.

    Why this sticks. Compounding looks slow, then sudden. Most of the growth comes at the end. That's why starting to save early matters so much, and why ignored debt can swamp you.

    The page you'd copy down.

    • Percent = out of 100. 15% = 0.15. Percent of a number: multiply. $60 × 0.15 = $9.

    09 Check

    Exam-style questions.

    Written the way a college exam would ask them. Each right answer on the first try is worth 10 xp.

    On the job: you're a financial analyst

    Your boss wants to know which of two companies is growing faster. Here are their yearly sales:

    • Company A: $2,000,000 last year, $2,500,000 this year.
    • Company B: $8,000,000 last year, $9,000,000 this year.
    • Company B's manager says, "We grew by $1,000,000, double Company A's $500,000. We're growing faster."

    Your task: work out each company's growth in percent. Is the manager right? If Company A keeps its growth rate, what will its sales be next year?

    See a model answer

    Company A: $2,500,000 − $2,000,000$2,000,000 = 0.25, so 25% growth.

    Company B: $9,000,000 − $8,000,000$8,000,000 = 0.125, so 12.5% growth.

    The manager's claim: B added more dollars, but from a much bigger start. In percent, A is growing twice as fast.

    Next year for A: $2,500,000 × 1.25 = $3,125,000, if the 25% rate holds.
