Unit 10 · Lesson 3 Behavioral finance

You knew the math. Your brain had other plans.

The story so far You took the $1,000 bonus now, just like the math said. A week later, $600 of it is gone on a new phone. The ad said "sale ends tonight," and the bonus felt like free money. Meanwhile, you still owe $600 on a credit card at 24% a year. Paying it off would have saved you $144 a year in interest. You knew that. So why didn't you? This lesson is about the mental shortcuts that trip up almost everyone, and the simple rules that beat them.

What

Behavioral finance studies how real people make money choices, mistakes included, instead of how a perfect calculator would.

Why

Knowing the math isn't enough. The same few mental traps cost people money on spending, debt and investing, over and over.

How

Learn to name each trap, then set up rules and defaults so the right choice happens without willpower.

Who

Financial advisors spend much of their time coaching clients through these traps. Behavioral economists study them. Product designers at banks and apps use them to build better defaults.

What you're looking at: two jars of $600 each. Your brain filed the bonus as "fun money" and the card balance as a separate problem. But a dollar is a dollar: using the bonus on the card would have saved $600 × 0.24 = $144 a year.

01 See it

Six traps and one fix.

Researchers have named dozens of money mistakes. These six show up the most, in spending, debt and investing. Once you can name a trap, it's much easier to step around it.

1. Mental accounting

Mental accounting is sorting money into separate "jars" in your head by where it came from. A bonus or tax refund feels like play money, even though it spends exactly like your paycheck.

In this example
Your bonus
$600 went to a phone as "fun money" while a $600 card balance kept costing 24%: $600 × 0.24 = $144 a year.

2. Loss aversion

Loss aversion means losing hurts more than winning feels good. Psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky found that a loss feels roughly twice as strong as a gain of the same size. That's why people hold losing stocks too long and panic-sell in drops (Lesson 5.6).

To judge a bet, find its expected value: what it pays on average. For a 50/50 coin flip, that's the win minus the loss, divided by 2.

Average result of a coin flip = win − loss2
In this example
Heads you win $150, tails you lose $100
$150 − $1002 = +$25 on average. A good bet, yet most people turn it down, because the $100 loss feels like $200.

How it feels (losses count double)

What you're looking at: a 50/50 coin flip where you always risk $100. Slide the prize. The top bar is the average result; the bottom bar is how it feels if a loss counts twice as much. The center line is zero: bars going right are good, left are bad.

3. Present bias

Present bias is wanting things now so badly that you ignore good deals for waiting. In Lesson 10.2 the math compared now and later fairly. Your brain doesn't.

In this example
$100 today or $110 in one week?
Many people grab the $100, passing up a 10% return in one week. But offered $100 in 52 weeks or $110 in 53 weeks, most choose to wait. Same one-week gap, different answer.

4. Anchoring

Anchoring is when the first number you see sticks and shapes how you judge everything after it. Stores and car dealers use it all the time.

In this example
"Was $200, now $120"
The $200 anchor makes $120 feel like a steal: $80 saved, 40% off. But if you weren't planning to buy it, you didn't save $80. You spent $120.

5. Herd behavior

Herd behavior is following the crowd. When everyone is buying something that's shooting up, it feels risky to miss out. Usually the crowd shows up late, after the price has already jumped.

In this example
A hyped stock
It climbs from $20 to $60 and your friends are all in. You buy at $60. It falls back to $30: $30 − $60$60 = −0.50, a 50% loss.

6. Overconfidence

Overconfidence is thinking you're better at predicting than you are. A few lucky wins make it worse. In investing, it shows up as trading too much.

In this example
A famous study
Researchers Brad Barber and Terrance Odean tracked about 66,000 households from 1991 to 1996. The ones who traded the most earned 11.4% a year, while the market returned 17.9%. Trading more cost them money.

The fix: make good choices automatic

Willpower runs out. A default doesn't. If the right move happens unless you stop it, the traps can't get in the way.

In this example
Retirement plans
Economists Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea studied a company that switched new workers to automatic 401(k) sign-up. Participation jumped from about 37% to 86%. Same people, same plan; only the default changed.
Your bonus, next time
Rule: windfalls pay off high-interest debt first. That $600 goes to the card, saving $144 a year.

    04 Break it down

    The logic, step by step.

    Before any big money choice, run these three checks.

    Step 1: Pause

    Wait 24 hours before any unplanned buy over a set amount
    In this example
    The phone
    "Sale ends tonight" is built to beat this step. A day later, the $600 phone may not feel urgent.

    Step 2: Name the trap

    Ask: am I sorting money into jars, fearing a loss, rushing, anchored, following the crowd, or overconfident?
    In this example
    The bonus
    Mental accounting ("it's extra money") plus present bias ("I want it now").

    Step 3: Do the math, then set a rule

    Compare the real costs, then turn the answer into a default
    In this example
    Phone vs. card
    Paying the card saves $144 a year. Rule: windfalls go to high-interest debt first.

    Worked example: should you take the bet?

    1. The bet. A 50/50 coin flip: win $150 or lose $100.
    2. Pause and name the trap. Saying no right away is loss aversion: the $100 loss feels like $200.
    3. Do the math. $150 − $1002 = +$25 on average.
    4. Check you can afford the loss. Only if losing $100 won't hurt your bills or emergency fund.
    5. The lesson. Small bets with a positive average, repeated, add up. That's the idea behind staying invested through ups and downs.
    05 Nature link

    Monkeys feel it too.

    Yale researchers taught capuchin monkeys to trade tokens for apple slices. One trader showed one slice and sometimes added a second. Another showed two slices and sometimes took one away. On average, both gave the same amount. The monkeys strongly preferred the trader who added, and avoided the one who took away. Even monkeys hate losses.

    What you're looking at: an illustration of the experiment, not data. The trader on the left starts with one slice and sometimes adds one. The trader on the right starts with two and sometimes takes one away. The monkeys went to the left trader far more often.

    Why this sticks. If capuchins share our fear of losses, it's not a flaw you can simply think your way out of. That's why rules and defaults work better than willpower.

    09 Check

    Exam-style questions.

    Written the way a college exam would ask them. Each right answer on the first try is worth 10 xp.

    On the job: you're a financial advisor

    A client calls you, upset. Here's the situation:

    • Their stock index fund fell 20%, from $50,000 to $40,000.
    • Their coworkers are all selling, and they want to sell everything today.
    • They don't need the money for 25 years, and they have a full emergency fund.

    Your task: which traps are at work? What gain would the fund need to get back to $50,000? What would you tell your client?

    See a model answer

    The traps: loss aversion (the $10,000 drop feels huge) and herd behavior (coworkers are selling).

    The comeback math: $50,000 − $40,000$40,000 = 0.25, a 25% gain to get back to even (Lesson 10.1).

    What selling does: it turns a paper loss into a real one, and they'd likely miss the recovery. Past drops have recovered, though there's no guarantee about timing.

    What to say: "You don't need this money for 25 years, and your emergency fund covers surprises. Let's stick to the plan you made when you were calm." A written plan and automatic monthly investing are the defaults that protect them.
