Unit 3 · Lesson 2 Budgeting and saving

Your car breaks down. Who pays?

The story so far You work full-time at the bike shop. $2,400 lands in your account each month, and your budget gives every dollar a job: $1,200 for needs, $720 for wants and $480 for savings. Now the question is what the $480 is for. One morning your car won't start and the repair shop says $450. If you have savings set aside, it's an annoying day. If you don't, it goes on a credit card at 24% interest. This lesson shows how to build the money that makes surprises boring.

What

An emergency fund is money you keep only for real surprises, like a repair, a medical bill or losing your job.

Why

Without one, every surprise becomes debt. With one, a $450 repair costs you $450, not $450 plus interest.

How

Add up a month of needs, multiply by three to six, and save toward it every month in a separate savings account.

Who

Financial coaches and financial planners help people set the goal. Bank tellers and personal bankers open the savings accounts that hold it.

What you're looking at: a sketch of an emergency fund as a jar of savings under a rain cloud. The cloud is a surprise bill; the jar is the money set aside so the bill doesn't become debt.

01 See it

How big, how fast, and where it lives.

Four questions build an emergency fund: what it's for, how much to save, how long it takes, and where to keep it. We'll answer each with your bike shop budget.

What it's for

An emergency fund is savings you only touch for something that is needed, urgent and unexpected. A car repair you need to get to work counts. Concert tickets, a sale, or a new phone when the old one still works don't. Neither do bills you know are coming, like car insurance due every six months: save for those a little each month in your budget.

How common are surprises? In a 2025 Federal Reserve survey, only 63% of U.S. adults said they could pay a surprise $400 bill with cash or savings. The other 37% would have to borrow, sell something, or couldn't pay at all.

In this example
Emergency
The $450 car repair: you need the car for work (needed), it broke today (urgent), and you didn't plan for it (unexpected).

How much to save

Size the fund by your monthly needs, the bills you must pay to live and work, not your whole income. Most planners suggest three to six months of needs. Three months fits someone with a steady job; aim for six if your pay changes month to month, if you're the only income in your home, or if your kind of job takes longer to replace.

Don't wait for the full amount to feel safe. A good first goal is $400, the size of the surprise bill in the Fed survey.

In this example
Monthly needs
Rent $900 + groceries $200 + gas $60 + phone $40 = $1,200.
Three months
$1,200 × 3 = $3,600. That's your goal.
Six months
$1,200 × 6 = $7,200, if your pay were less steady.

How long it takes

Divide the goal by what you save each month. Set the transfer to happen automatically on payday, so the money moves before you can spend it.

In this example
First $400
$400$480 a month = 0.83, so less than one month.
Full $3,600
$3,600$480 a month = 7.5 months.
Months of needs to cover

What you're looking at: an emergency fund planner. The jar is your goal (months of needs). The row of blocks is each month of saving; the jar fills as the months go by, and the line marks the $400 first goal. The numbers are an example; saving interest isn't counted.

Where to keep it

Keep it in a savings account, separate from checking, so you don't spend it by accident. It should be easy to reach in a day or two, and safe: savings at an insured bank is protected by the FDIC up to $250,000. Don't keep it in stocks. Stock prices can drop a lot in a bad year, and that's often the same year people lose jobs. The next lesson shows how a high-yield savings account can pay you more while it waits.

In this example
Your setup
Checking for bills, a separate savings account for the fund, and an automatic $480 transfer every month on payday.

How long it lasts, and refilling it

Your runway is how many months the fund could pay your needs if your paycheck stopped. When you use the fund, that's what it's for. Then refill it: pause other saving goals and send your monthly savings back into the fund until it's full.

In this example
Runway
$3,600$1,200 a month = 3 months of rent, food, gas and phone with no paycheck.
After the repair
$3,600 − $450 = $3,150 left. Refill time: $450$480 a month = 0.94, about one month.

Worked example: a six-month fund

  1. Needs. A friend, Rosa, sells cars and is paid by commission, so her pay changes every month. Her needs are $1,500 a month.
  2. Months. Uneven pay means she aims for six months.
  3. Goal. $1,500 × 6 = $9,000.
  4. Time. She has $1,000 and saves $400 a month: $9,000 − $1,000$400 = 20 months.
  5. Runway when full. $9,000$1,500 = 6 months.
05 Nature link

A camel's hump is a savings account.

A camel's hump isn't full of water. It's fat, up to about 80 pounds of it. When food is scarce in the desert, the camel lives off that stored fat and the hump shrinks and slumps over. When it eats well again, the hump fills back up.

What you're looking at: an illustration, not data. The same camel with a full hump (left) and after living off it in a lean stretch (right).

Why this sticks. The camel doesn't carry its fat for fun; it's there so a hard week doesn't become a crisis. Your fund is the same. Fill it in good months, live on it when a paycheck stops or a bill hits, then fill it again.

07 Together

Grab a friend or a study group.

Emergency or not?

Take turns naming something that costs money, like a flat tire or a sale on shoes. The group votes: emergency or not? Use the test: needed, urgent, unexpected.

Size each other's jar

Each person lists their monthly needs. Partners figure out the three-month and six-month goals for each other and which one fits.

The $400 question

Ask: "If a $400 bill showed up tomorrow, how would you pay it?" Compare answers with the 63% from the Fed survey.

09 Check

Exam-style questions.

Written the way a college exam would ask them. Each right answer on the first try is worth 10 xp.

On the job: you're the financial coach

A new client, Leo, sits down with you. Here are his numbers:

  • He takes home $2,000 a month.
  • His needs (rent, food, bus pass and phone) cost $1,100 a month.
  • He has $300 in savings and no other savings.
  • He has a steady job, and he wants to start putting $200 a month into stocks.

Your task: tell Leo what he should do with the $200 first, how big his emergency fund goal should be, and how many months it will take.

See a model answer

Fund first, then stocks. $300 won't cover a surprise like a car repair or a few weeks without pay, and stocks can drop right when he'd need the money. So the $200 should go into savings first.

The goal: his job is steady, so three months of needs: $1,100 × 3 = $3,300.

The time: he already has $300, so he needs $3,300 − $300 = $3,000 more. At $200 a month: $3,000$200 = 15 months. He'll pass the $400 first goal next month. Once the fund is full, the $200 can go to stocks.
