Unit 4 · Budgeting and banking

Give every dollar a job

You just got your first real paycheck: $2,400 a month after taxes. Rent alone eats $900. By the end of this lesson you'll have a plan for every dollar, and you'll have survived a month of real life with it.

❓What am I doing?

Splitting the money you take home into planned jobs (needs, wants and savings) before you spend it.

💡Why does it matter?

Without a plan, surprise costs end up on a credit card at 20%+ interest. With one, they come out of savings you already set aside.

⚙️How does it work?

Two tools: the 50/30/20 rule gives you a quick target split, and zero-based budgeting assigns every single dollar until $0 is left unplanned.

🧑‍💼Who does this for a living?

Financial planners build these budgets for clients. Inside companies, FP&A analysts (financial planning and analysis) do the same thing at scale, with millions of dollars and dozens of departments.

🎧 Hear it Your style

Listen to the walkthrough

About 2 minutes. Each line lights up as it's read, so you can follow along or close your eyes.

Here's the big idea. A budget isn't a punishment. It's a plan that tells your money where to go before it disappears.

Start with what you actually take home after taxes. In our example that's twenty-four hundred dollars a month.

The fifty, thirty, twenty rule is a quick target. Fifty percent goes to needs, the things you'd be in trouble without: rent, food, getting to work.

Thirty percent goes to wants. That's fun money: eating out, games, clothes you'd like but don't need.

And twenty percent goes to your future: an emergency fund first, then investing or paying down debt faster.

Zero-based budgeting goes one step further. You keep assigning dollars until income minus everything you planned equals exactly zero.

Zero doesn't mean you spend it all. Savings counts as a job. It just means no dollar is wandering around without a plan.

Here's why this matters. When your car breaks down, a budget with an emergency fund pays for it calmly. Without one, the repair goes on a credit card, and it keeps costing you interest for months.

Companies do the exact same thing. Every department gets a budget, and analysts compare what was planned against what actually happened. You're learning the job right now.

    🧮 Break it down Your style

    The logic, step by step

    The 50/30/20 rule

    Needs = 0.50 × take-home pay
    Wants = 0.30 × take-home pay
    Savings = 0.20 × take-home pay

    Zero-based budgeting

    Take-home pay − (sum of every planned category) = $0

    Worked example: $2,400 take-home

    1. Find the targets. Needs 0.50 × 2,400 = $1,200. Wants 0.30 × 2,400 = $720. Savings 0.20 × 2,400 = $480.
    2. List fixed needs first. Rent $900, phone $50, transport $120, groceries $280. Total = $1,350. That's $150 over the needs target, which is normal when rent is high.
    3. Take the overage from wants, not savings. Wants drop from $720 to $570. Savings stay at $480.
    4. Split savings by priority. Emergency fund first ($330), investing second ($150), until you have about 3 months of needs saved.
    5. Check the zero. 2,400 − (1,350 + 570 + 480) = 0. Every dollar has a job.
    Go deeper (college level): why 50/30/20 is a heuristic, not a law

    A heuristic is a rule of thumb that's good enough most of the time. The rule was popularized by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi in All Your Worth (2005). It ignores location: in high-rent cities, needs often run 60% or more. It also ignores goals: someone paying off a 24% credit card should push "savings" well above 20%, because paying off 24% debt is a guaranteed 24% return.

    In a college finance class, this links to opportunity cost (every dollar spent on a want is a dollar not compounding) and to liquidity (an emergency fund is cash you can reach right away without selling investments at a bad time).

    🌿 Nature link Your style

    Your budget is a watershed

    Your paycheck is rainfall. It splits into streams, and the savings stream fills a reservoir. In a drought (a surprise bill), towns with a full reservoir are fine. Towns without one go thirsty, or borrow water at a high price.

    Why the stream widths matter: rivers carve deeper channels where more water flows. Spending habits work the same way. The categories you feed the most become the easiest places for money to keep going. A budget is you choosing where the channels get carved.

    📝 Notes Your style

    Summary notes

    • Take-home pay (net pay) is what's left after taxes and deductions. Budgets start here, not from your salary.
    • Needs are costs you'd face real trouble without: housing, groceries, transport to work, insurance, minimum debt payments.
    • Wants are improvements to your life you could pause: eating out, entertainment, upgrades.
    • Savings includes your emergency fund, investing, and extra debt payments above the minimum.
    • 50/30/20 is a quick target split. Adjust it to your city and goals.
    • Zero-based budgeting: income − all planned categories = $0. Savings is a category.
    • Order of priority: needs → minimum debt payments → starter emergency fund → high-interest debt → wants and investing.

    Exam-style quiz

    These are written the way a college exam would ask them. Each correct first try is worth 10 XP.

    💼 Job task: you're the financial planner

    Your client, Jordan, takes home $4,000 a month. Jordan spends $2,300 on needs (rent is $1,700), $1,400 on wants, $300 on savings, and has a $3,000 credit card balance at 24% APR.

    Your task: compare Jordan to 50/30/20, name the biggest problem, and suggest a new split in dollars.

    Show a model answer

    Targets: needs $2,000, wants $1,200, savings $800. Jordan is $300 over on needs, $200 over on wants, and $500 under on savings.

    Biggest problem: the 24% credit card. That balance costs about $60 a month in interest (3,000 × 0.24 ÷ 12), which is more than any savings account will pay.

    Suggested split: needs stay $2,300 for now (rent is locked by the lease), wants cut to $900, savings rise to $800, with $600 to the card and $200 to a starter emergency fund. The card is paid off in about 5 to 6 months, and then the $600 moves to the emergency fund and investing.
