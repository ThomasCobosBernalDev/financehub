Unit 4 · Lesson 1 Budgeting

Give every dollar a job.

The story so far You're 18 and just started full-time at a bike shop for $18 an hour. After taxes (that's Unit 3), $2,400 lands in your bank account every month. Rent is $900. So where does the rest go?

What

Split your take-home pay into planned jobs before you spend any of it.

Why

Without a plan, surprises go on a credit card at 20%+ interest.

How

Use 50/30/20 as a target, then zero-based budgeting to plan every dollar.

Who

Financial planners do this for clients. FP&A analysts do it for whole companies.

    04 Break it down your style

    The logic, step by step.

    The 50/30/20 rule

    Needs = 0.50 × take-home pay
    Wants = 0.30 × take-home pay
    Savings = 0.20 × take-home pay

    Zero-based budgeting

    Take-home pay − every planned category = $0

    Worked example: $2,400 take-home

    1. Find the targets. Needs 0.50 × 2,400 = $1,200. Wants 0.30 × 2,400 = $720. Savings 0.20 × 2,400 = $480.
    2. List fixed needs first. Rent $900, phone $50, transport $120, groceries $280. That's $1,350, which is $150 over the target. That's normal when rent is high.
    3. Take the overage from wants, not savings. Wants drop from $720 to $570. Savings stay at $480.
    4. Split savings by priority. Emergency fund first ($330), investing second ($150), until you have about 3 months of needs saved.
    5. Check the zero. 2,400 − (1,350 + 570 + 480) = 0. Every dollar has a job.
    Go deeper: why 50/30/20 is a rule of thumb, not a law

    A rule of thumb (economists say heuristic) is good enough most of the time. This one was popularized by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi in All Your Worth (2005). It ignores location: in expensive cities, needs often run 60% or more. It also ignores goals: if you're paying off a 24% credit card, push "savings" well above 20%, because paying off 24% debt is a guaranteed 24% return.

    In a college finance class this connects to opportunity cost (every dollar spent on a want is a dollar not compounding) and liquidity (an emergency fund is cash you can reach right away without selling investments at a bad time).

    05 Nature link your style

    Your budget is a watershed.

    Your paycheck is rain. It splits into streams, and the savings stream fills a reservoir. When a drought hits (a surprise bill), towns with a full reservoir are fine. Towns without one go thirsty, or buy water at a terrible price.

    Why stream width matters. Rivers carve deeper channels where more water flows. Spending habits work the same way: the categories you feed most become the easiest places for money to keep going. A budget is you deciding where the channels get carved.

    06 Notes your style

    The page you'd copy down.

    • Take-home pay (net pay) is what's left after taxes and deductions. Budgets start here.
    • Needs: housing, groceries, getting to work, insurance, minimum debt payments.
    • Wants: things that improve life but could be paused.
    • Savings: emergency fund, investing, extra debt payments.
    • 50/30/20 is a quick target. Adjust it for your city and goals.
    • Zero-based: income − every planned category = $0. Savings counts as a category.
    • Order: needs → minimum payments → starter emergency fund → high-interest debt → wants and investing.

    07 Together your style

    Grab a friend or a study group.

    Roommate role-play

    Two roommates split $1,800 rent and $150 utilities. One takes home $2,400, the other $3,600. One of you argues for 50/50, the other for splitting by income (40/60). Agree on one, then both build a 50/30/20 budget with it. Who ends up over their needs target?

    Three-minute debate

    "The 50/30/20 rule doesn't work in expensive cities." Take opposite sides and use real rent from a city you'd want to live in. Whoever changes the other person's mind with a number wins.

    Survivor showdown

    Both play Month Survivor and compare grades. Explain one choice you made differently and why. Teaching your reasoning to someone else is one of the fastest ways to lock it in.

    09 Check

    Exam-style questions.

    Written the way a college exam would ask them. Each right answer on the first try is worth 10 xp.

    On the job: you're the financial planner

    Your client Jordan takes home $4,000 a month. Jordan spends $2,300 on needs (rent is $1,700), $1,400 on wants and $300 on savings, and has a $3,000 credit card balance at 24% APR.

    Your task: compare Jordan to 50/30/20, name the biggest problem, and suggest a new split in dollars.

    See a model answer

    Targets: needs $2,000, wants $1,200, savings $800. Jordan is $300 over on needs, $200 over on wants and $500 under on savings.

    Biggest problem: the 24% credit card. It costs about $60 a month in interest (3,000 × 0.24 ÷ 12), more than any savings account pays.

    New split: needs stay $2,300 for now (the lease locks rent), wants drop to $900, and savings rise to $800: $600 to the card and $200 to a starter emergency fund. The card is gone in about 5 to 6 months, then that $600 moves to the emergency fund and investing.
