Unit 1 · Lesson 2 Supply and demand

Why prices are what they are.

The story so far Your summer of fixing bikes paid off: you've got a stack of them ready and a table at the Saturday market. Each one cost you about $30 in parts. Charge too much and people walk past. Charge too little and they're gone in ten minutes. So what's the right price?

What

Find the price where the number people want to buy matches the number for sale.

Why

Every price you'll ever pay, from rent to stock shares, is set by this tug-of-war.

How

Buyers want more when prices fall. Sellers offer more when prices rise. They meet at equilibrium.

Who

Pricing analysts set prices for companies. Commodity traders bet on where prices go next.

    04 Break it down your style

    The logic, step by step.

    The two laws

    Law of demand: price ↑ → quantity demanded ↓
    Law of supply: price ↑ → quantity supplied ↑

    Equilibrium

    Quantity demanded = quantity supplied

    Worked example: solve it like an exam

    1. Write the curves. Demand: Qd = 100 − P. Supply: Qs = 2P − 20. (P is price in dollars, Q is bikes.)
    2. Set them equal. 100 − P = 2P − 20, so 120 = 3P and P = $40.
    3. Find the quantity. Plug P back in: Q = 100 − 40 = 60 bikes. Check with supply: 2(40) − 20 = 60. ✓
    4. Test a price below. At $30, Qd = 70 and Qs = 40. Buyers want 30 more than exist: a shortage of 30. Prices get pushed up.
    5. Test a price above. At $50, Qd = 50 and Qs = 80. A surplus of 30 unsold bikes. Prices get pushed down.

    Shift or slide?

    A change in the bike's own price slides you along a curve. Anything else (income, tastes, prices of related goods, number of buyers or sellers, costs) shifts the whole curve. Exams test this constantly.

    Go deeper: elasticity, price ceilings and price floors

    Price elasticity of demand measures how much buyers react to price: % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price. If a 10% price rise cuts sales 20%, elasticity is −2 (elastic: people have options). Gas and insulin are inelastic (people buy them anyway). Elastic goods punish price hikes, which is why your stall's pricing mattered so much.

    Price ceilings are legal maximums set below equilibrium, like rent control. They cause shortages: more people want apartments at that rent than exist. Price floors are legal minimums set above equilibrium, like a minimum wage. They can cause surpluses (unemployment) in the textbook model, though real-world evidence is debated, and that debate is a classic exam essay.

    Consumer and producer surplus. A buyer who'd pay $150 for a bike priced at $100 gets $50 of "consumer surplus." A seller whose cost is $30 gets $70 of "producer surplus." Equilibrium maximizes the two added together. You'll see these as triangles on a graph.

    05 Nature link your style

    Bees find the equilibrium too.

    Put a big patch of flowers next to a small one and watch the bees. At first they crowd the big patch. Soon it's so busy that each bee gets less nectar, so some drift to the small patch. They keep shifting until every bee gets about the same reward, wherever it is.

    Ecologists call it the "ideal free distribution." Economists call it equilibrium. Same idea: when one spot pays better, newcomers rush in until the advantage disappears. It's why a hot new food truck soon has three copycats parked next to it, and why easy profits in any market never last long.

    06 Notes your style

    The page you'd copy down.

    • Demand: how much buyers want at each price. Slopes down.
    • Supply: how much sellers offer at each price. Slopes up.
    • Equilibrium: the price where quantity demanded = quantity supplied. The market "clears."
    • Shortage: price below equilibrium. Buyers want more than exists. Price tends to rise.
    • Surplus: price above equilibrium. More for sale than buyers want. Price tends to fall.
    • Demand shifters: income, tastes, number of buyers, prices of substitutes and complements, expectations.
    • Supply shifters: input costs, technology, number of sellers, expectations, taxes.
    • Own price changes = movement along the curve. Anything else = shift of the curve.

    07 Together your style

    Grab a friend or a study group.

    The concert ticket auction

    Write down the most you'd pay for a ticket to your favorite artist. One person is the seller with 3 tickets. Start at $300 and lower the price until 3 people say "buy." That price is your group's equilibrium. Who got consumer surplus, and how much?

    Shift or slide?

    Take turns calling out headlines ("Gas hits $6 a gallon," "A new e-bike costs half as much"). The others race to say which curve moves, which way, and what happens to price. Loser buys the snacks.

    Stall showdown

    Both play Market Stall. Compare your prices on the Saturday the big-box store opened. Who read the news better, and how much profit did it cost the other person?

    08 On your own your style

    Just you and the prices around you.

    These stay on this device. Nobody else sees them.

    Think of something whose price jumped recently: sneakers, eggs, concert tickets, rent. Was it demand going up, supply going down, or both?
    What's the most you'd pay for something you use every day? How far above the current price is that? That gap is your consumer surplus.
    Your skills are a product too, and employers are the buyers. What could you learn that very few people around you can do? That's how you'd raise your own price.

    09 Check

    Exam-style questions.

    Written the way a college exam would ask them. Each right answer on the first try is worth 10 xp.

    On the job: you're the pricing analyst

    A sneaker brand is launching a shoe. Market research estimates demand of Q = 50,000 − 200P pairs. The factory can make 20,000 pairs for the launch, no more.

    Your task: what launch price sells exactly 20,000 pairs? What happens to resale prices if the brand prices it at $120 instead?

    See a model answer

    Set demand equal to supply: 50,000 − 200P = 20,000, so 200P = 30,000 and P = $150.

    At $120: demand is 50,000 − 200(120) = 26,000 pairs, but only 20,000 exist. That's a shortage of 6,000. Those buyers bid on resale sites, pushing resale prices toward $150 or higher. The brand leaves about $30 a pair, or $600,000, on the table, and resellers collect it.

    Why a brand might do it anyway: sold-out launches and long lines build hype. That's a marketing choice with a real, measurable cost, and your job is to put a number on it.
