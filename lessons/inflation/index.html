Unit 1 · Lesson 3 Inflation and GDP

Why your money shrinks.

The story so far Your bike stall did well. Now you have real savings in a drawer, and your grandpa tells you a movie ticket cost about $2 when he was 17. Today it's about $11. Your cash didn't go anywhere. So why will it buy less every year?

What

Measure how fast prices rise (inflation) and how big the economy is (GDP).

Why

Money that sits still loses value. A raise below inflation is really a pay cut.

How

Track the price of a basket of everyday things, and adjust every number for it: real vs. nominal.

Who

Government economists measure it. Macro strategists at banks forecast it for investors.

    04 Break it down your style

    The logic, step by step.

    Inflation rate

    Inflation = (CPIthis year − CPIlast year) ÷ CPIlast year

    Moving a price through time

    Price in today's dollars = old price × (CPItoday ÷ CPIthen)

    Real vs. nominal

    Real rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation

    Worked example: was it a raise?

    1. Get the CPI. The CPI averaged 292.7 in 2022 and 304.7 in 2023.
    2. Find inflation. (304.7 − 292.7) ÷ 292.7 = 0.041, so prices rose about 4.1% from 2022 to 2023.
    3. Check the raise. Your pay went from $18.00 to $18.36 an hour, a nominal raise of 2%.
    4. Make it real. Real raise ≈ 2% − 4.1% = −2.1%. You got more dollars but can buy less. That's a pay cut in disguise.
    5. Flip it to a target. To keep up, you'd need at least $18.00 × 1.041 = $18.74.

    GDP

    GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)
    Real GDP = nominal GDP ÷ price index × 100

    If nominal GDP grows 5% but prices rose 3%, the economy really grew only about 2%. Two quarters of falling real GDP in a row is a common rule of thumb for a recession.

    Go deeper: what causes inflation, the Fisher equation and CPI's blind spots

    Two main causes. Demand-pull: people and the government try to spend more than the economy can produce, so prices get bid up (2021, after stimulus). Cost-push: something makes producing stuff more expensive, like an oil shock (1973 and 1979). Over long periods, inflation tracks how fast the money supply grows faster than output. That's the quantity theory: MV = PQ.

    The exact Fisher equation is (1 + nominal) = (1 + real) × (1 + inflation). At 8% nominal and 5% inflation, real = 1.08 ÷ 1.05 − 1 = 2.86%, a bit less than the 3% shortcut. Use the exact version when rates are high.

    CPI isn't perfect. It can overstate inflation because people switch to cheaper substitutes and products get better (a $1,000 phone today does far more than one from 2010). Economists adjust for quality, and some argue it still misses things. Your personal inflation depends on what you actually buy: rent and tuition have risen faster than the average.

    Why not zero inflation? Most central banks aim for about 2%. A little inflation makes it easier for wages and prices to adjust, and avoids deflation, where falling prices make people delay purchases and debts get heavier, which can spiral into a slump.

    05 Nature link your style

    Cash in a drawer is a pond in summer.

    A pond with no stream feeding it loses water to the sun every day. You don't notice from one day to the next, but by August the shore has crept in. Inflation evaporates money the same way. Interest is the stream. If the stream runs slower than the evaporation, the pond still shrinks.

    Why this sticks. Hydrologists measure evaporation against inflow to know if a lake is healthy. Do the same with your money: compare what it earns against what inflation takes. That one habit is the whole reason people invest instead of hoarding cash.

    06 Notes your style

    The page you'd copy down.

    • Inflation: a general rise in prices across the economy. Each dollar buys less: lower purchasing power.
    • CPI: the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services, tracked monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics.
    • Inflation rate = % change in CPI. The Fed targets about 2% a year.
    • Nominal = in raw dollars. Real = adjusted for inflation. Real ≈ nominal − inflation.
    • Rule of 70: years for prices to double ≈ 70 ÷ inflation rate. At 3%, about 23 years.
    • GDP = market value of all final goods and services made in a country in a year = C + I + G + (X − M).
    • Real GDP strips out price changes. Growth in real GDP = the economy actually making more.
    • Recession: a broad, lasting fall in economic activity. Rule of thumb: two quarters of falling real GDP.

    07 Together your style

    Grab a friend or a study group.

    Interview the past

    Ask a parent or grandparent what they paid for a car, their first apartment and a gallon of gas, and when. Convert each to today's dollars with the formula. Which one grew faster than inflation, and why might that be?

    Build your own CPI

    Each person lists five things they buy every month and today's prices. Look up (or guess) prices from five years ago. Whose personal inflation rate is highest? Students who pay rent usually win this one.

    Era showdown

    Each of you plays Beat Inflation in a different era. Compare: in which decade was cash in a drawer the worst idea? Explain it to each other using real vs. nominal.

    09 Check

    Exam-style questions.

    Written the way a college exam would ask them. Each right answer on the first try is worth 10 xp.

    On the job: you're the macro strategist

    A client keeps $200,000 in a savings account earning 0.5%. Inflation is running at 4%. The client says, "At least I'm not losing money."

    Your task: show the client, in dollars, how much buying power they lose in one year. Then explain in two sentences why "not losing money" isn't true.

    See a model answer

    Real return ≈ 0.5% − 4% = −3.5%. On $200,000, that's about $7,000 of buying power lost in a year (exactly: 200,000 × 1.005 ÷ 1.04 ≈ $193,269, a loss of $6,731).

    The point: the balance goes up by $1,000, but prices go up by $8,000. The client has more dollars and can buy less with them, so in real terms they are losing money every month the cash sits there.
