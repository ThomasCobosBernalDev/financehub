Unit 11 · Lesson 1 Starting a business

$40 a tune-up. But when do you start making money?

The story so far After a few years at the bike shop, you know bikes. So you start a weekend side business: tune-ups at the farmers market. You charge $40 a tune-up, and the parts for each one cost you $10. The market spot costs $200 a month, and tools and insurance add $100 a month. Your first month, you do 6 tune-ups, take in $240, and still lose money. How many tune-ups do you need before you make a single dollar? This lesson shows you how to find out before you open.

What

Profit is what's left after every cost is paid. The break-even point is the number of sales where you stop losing money.

Why

Most new businesses fail because the numbers never worked. Ten minutes of math can tell you if an idea can pay.

How

Split costs into fixed and variable, find what each sale adds, then divide.

Who

Small-business owners run these numbers every month. Small-business advisors check them for new owners. Pricing analysts and financial analysts at big companies do the same math with bigger numbers.

What you're looking at: one $40 tune-up split into its $10 of parts and the $30 left over. Each $30 goes into the jar for your $300 of monthly fixed costs. It takes 10 tune-ups to fill the jar. After that, every tune-up's $30 is profit.

01 See it

The numbers behind every business.

A food truck, a lawn service and a tech company all run on the same few numbers: what comes in, what goes out, and how many sales it takes to cover the bills.

Revenue

Revenue is all the money customers pay you, before you pay any costs. Some people call it sales.

Revenue = price × number sold
In this example
A busy month
25 tune-ups × $40 = $1,000 of revenue.

Fixed and variable costs

Fixed costs stay the same whether you sell nothing or a lot. Variable costs go up with every sale.

In this example
Fixed
Market spot $200 + tools and insurance $100 = $300 a month, even if you do zero tune-ups.
Variable
Parts: $10 per tune-up. 25 tune-ups = 25 × $10 = $250.

Profit

Profit is what's left after you pay every cost. If it's negative, it's a loss.

Profit = revenue − fixed costs − variable costs
In this example
25 tune-ups
$1,000 − $300 − $250 = $450 profit.
Your first month, 6 tune-ups
$240 − $300 − $60 = −$120, a $120 loss.

Contribution margin

The contribution margin is what each sale leaves over after its own costs. That leftover pays the fixed costs first, then becomes profit.

Contribution margin = price − variable cost per sale
In this example
One tune-up
$40 − $10 = $30. Every tune-up adds $30 toward the $300 of fixed costs.

The break-even point

The break-even point is the number of sales where you stop losing money and haven't made any yet. Below it, you lose money. Above it, you make some. If the answer has a decimal, round up: you can't sell part of a tune-up.

Break-even sales = fixed costscontribution margin
In this example
Your tune-ups
$300$30 = 10 tune-ups a month. Tune-up number 11 is your first dollar of profit.

Profit margin

Profit margin shows how much of each dollar of sales you keep. It lets you compare a small business with a big one.

Profit margin = profitrevenue
In this example
25 tune-ups
$450$1,000 = 0.45, a 45% profit margin. You keep 45 cents of every dollar.

Is a higher price always better?

A higher price adds more per sale, but some customers walk away (Lesson 8.2). What matters is the total.

In this example
At $40
25 customers: 25 × $30 − $300 = $450.
At $50
Only 18 customers: each adds $50 − $10 = $40, so 18 × $40 − $300 = $420. The higher price earns $30 less.

What you're looking at: your tune-up business with $300 of fixed costs and $10 of parts per tune-up. The green line is revenue; the orange line is total costs. Where they cross is the break-even point. The dashed line marks how many tune-ups you did.

    04 Break it down

    The logic, step by step.

    Before you start any business, run these three steps.

    Step 1: Sort your costs

    Ask of each cost: does it go up when I sell one more?
    In this example
    Fixed
    Market spot and tools: $300 a month.
    Variable
    Parts: $10 for each tune-up.

    Step 2: Find what each sale adds

    Contribution margin = price − variable cost
    In this example
    One tune-up
    $40 − $10 = $30.

    Step 3: Divide, and round up

    Break-even = fixed costscontribution margin
    In this example
    Tune-ups
    $300$30 = 10 a month.

    Worked example: a bigger spot

    1. The offer. A better market spot costs $380 a month instead of $200. Your fixed costs become $380 + $100 = $480.
    2. What each sale adds. Still $40 − $10 = $30.
    3. New break-even. $480$30 = 16 tune-ups a month, up from 10.
    4. Is it worth it? Only if the better spot brings at least 6 more tune-ups a month: 6 × $30 = $180, the extra rent.
    5. The lesson. Every new fixed cost raises the bar. Know how many extra sales it needs before you sign.
    05 Nature link

    The hummingbird's energy budget.

    A hummingbird burns energy so fast that it has to feed many times an hour just to break even. Its heartbeat and wingbeats are a fixed cost it pays every minute. On cold nights, when it can't feed, it drops into torpor, a deep sleep that slows its heart and lowers its body temperature, cutting its energy use sharply until morning.

    What you're looking at: an illustration, not data. On the left, a hummingbird feeding during the day. On the right, the same bird at night in torpor, its body slowed down to save energy.

    Why this sticks. Like a business, a hummingbird has costs it pays no matter what. When it can't bring in enough, it survives by cutting those fixed costs. Owners do the same in a slow season: smaller spot, fewer subscriptions, shorter hours.

    09 Check

    Exam-style questions.

    Written the way a college exam would ask them. Each right answer on the first try is worth 10 xp.

    On the job: you're a small-business advisor

    A client runs a food truck and asks if raising prices is a good idea. Here are the numbers:

    • Fixed costs: $6,000 a month (truck loan, permits, insurance).
    • Average meal: $12. Food and packaging: $4 a meal.
    • Sales now: 1,200 meals a month.
    • The idea: raise the average meal to $14. They expect to lose 10% of customers, so 1,080 meals.

    Your task: find the break-even point and the monthly profit now. Then work out the profit at the new price. What do you advise?

    See a model answer

    Now: each meal adds $12 − $4 = $8. Break-even is $6,000$8 = 750 meals a month. Profit: 1,200 × $8 − $6,000 = $3,600.

    At $14: each meal adds $14 − $4 = $10. Break-even drops to $6,000$10 = 600 meals. Profit: 1,080 × $10 − $6,000 = $4,800.

    Advice: the raise adds $1,200 a month, even after losing 120 customers. Test it for a month and track the real sales, because the 10% loss is a guess.
