Unit 1 · Lesson 3 Bank fees

Small fees add up. Almost all of them are avoidable.

The story so far You opened checking at Riverbend Community Credit Union and savings at Peak. Riverbend just mailed your debit card. It's Wednesday: checking has $50, savings has $300, and your next $600 paycheck lands Friday. At the register the clerk asks, "Debit or credit?" And the Riverbend app asks, "Want overdraft coverage?"

What

A debit card spends straight from checking. Spend more than you have, and the bank may cover it and charge an overdraft fee, about $27.

Why

Banks still collected $5.8 billion in overdraft and bounced-payment fees in 2023. Nearly every one of those fees can be avoided.

How

Spend from your available balance, set a low-balance alert, keep a cushion, and choose about overdraft coverage on purpose.

Who

Bank product managers set fee rules. CFPB analysts study and regulate them. Fraud analysts catch stolen-card charges.

What you're looking at: a sketch, not data. The tags are 2025's average bank fees: $27 overdraft, $16.82 bounced payment, $4.86 ATM and $5.47 monthly fee.

01 See it

A debit card spends money you have. Sometimes money you don't.

First, how a debit card pays. Then how an overdraft happens, what it costs, and the settings that stop it. Last, the other fees on the menu and what happens if your card is stolen.

How a debit card pays

A debit card pulls money straight out of checking. A credit card borrows the bank's money, and you pay it back later. Your app shows two numbers. The available balance is what you can spend right now. Your current balance doesn't yet count a pending transaction, a purchase that's approved but hasn't finished going through. A gas pump or hotel may put a hold on your money the same way.

Overdrafts: when the bank pays anyway

An overdraft happens when a purchase or bill is bigger than your available balance and the bank pays it anyway, so your account drops below $0. For covering it, the bank charges an overdraft fee. If the bank refuses and sends a bill payment back unpaid, it may charge an NSF fee, short for nonsufficient funds, and the company you owed may add a late fee. Since 2010, federal rules say a bank can't charge an overdraft fee on an ATM withdrawal or a one-time debit card purchase unless you opt in. If you don't, the card is simply declined, for free. Checks and automatic bill payments are different: they can still overdraw your account either way.

In this example
Overdraft
You have $50.00 available and buy a $70.00 textbook. Your balance becomes −$20.00.
Overdraft fee
Riverbend charges $27, close to 2025's average of $26.77. That $70 book really cost $70 + $27 = $97.
NSF fee
If your $45 phone autopay bounces instead, the average fee is $16.82, plus whatever late fee the phone company adds.
Opt in
If you never opted in, the textbook purchase is declined, and you pay $0.

One bad day

One overdraft often brings more, because every fee pushes you further below $0. The order the bank posts your charges matters too. Some banks used to post the largest charge first, which drains your balance fastest and turns more small purchases into overdrafts. In 2010, a federal judge ordered Wells Fargo to pay $203 million for doing exactly that. Set a starting balance, choose your settings, and watch one day post.

    The rest of the fee menu

    Overdrafts are the biggest fee, but not the only one. Here's what the average account charged in 2025, and the move that makes each one $0.

    $26.77Overdraft fee

    The bank paid when you were short. Dodge it: opt out, set alerts, keep a cushion.

    $16.82NSF fee

    A check or autopay bounced. Dodge it: move bill due dates to just after payday.

    $4.86Out-of-network ATM

    $3.22 to the ATM's owner plus $1.64 to your bank. Dodge it: your bank's ATMs, or cash back at a store.

    $5.47Monthly fee

    For basic checking that charges one. 95% of accounts are free or can be. Dodge it: direct deposit or a free account.

    What you're looking at: real data, the averages from Bankrate's 2025 Checking Account and ATM Fee Study.

    Debit or credit when a card is stolen?

    A charge you didn't make is an unauthorized charge. How much of it you could lose, your liability, depends on the card and on how fast you report it. With a credit card, federal law caps it at $50. With a debit card, the cap grows the longer you wait, and while the bank investigates, it's your own checking money that's missing. Pick a card and when you report a lost wallet.

    In this example
    Unauthorized charge
    Your wallet is stolen, and the thief spends $900 on your card.
    Liability
    On the debit card, report within 2 business days and you owe at most $50. Wait longer and it can be up to $500. On a credit card, it's $50 at most.
    Card that was stolen
    You report it
    You could loseThe bank covers

    What you're looking at: a calculator using the federal limits for a lost or stolen card: the Electronic Fund Transfer Act for debit cards and the Fair Credit Billing Act for credit cards. Card networks and many banks promise $0, but the law is the floor. If you don't report within 60 days of your statement, you can lose everything charged after that.

    Five fee shields

    Set these up once and most fees never reach you. The strongest is a cushion, money you never plan to spend that keeps you above $0. The second is overdraft protection: linking a savings account at the same bank, so it covers a shortfall instantly.

    In this example
    Cushion
    Start the day with $100 instead of $50, and all five charges clear, leaving $5.26 with $0 in fees.
    Overdraft protection
    Your savings is at Peak, a different bank. A transfer from another bank takes 1 to 3 business days, so it can't rescue you at the register. A Riverbend savings account linked to checking could, the same instant.
    • A low-balance alert

      Get a text when checking drops under $100, before it hits $0.

    • A cushion

      Keep $100 or so in checking that you treat as $0.

    • Overdraft protection

      Link savings at the same bank to cover a shortfall instantly.

    • Choose your opt-in

      Opted out, a short card purchase is declined, for $0, not $27.

    • Bills after payday

      Ask each company to set your due date a day or two after you're paid.

    04 Break it down

    The logic, step by step.

    Four calculations show what a fee really costs.

    Step 1: What you can spend

    Start from your current balance and take away everything still pending.

    Available = current balance − pending charges and holds
    In this example
    Wednesday
    $69.75 − $25.00 of pending gas = $44.75 you can really spend.

    Step 2: What an overdraft really costs

    Add the fee to what you bought.

    Real cost = purchase + fee
    In this example
    Textbook
    $70 + $27 = $97. A $5.25 coffee that overdraws costs $5.25 + $27 = $32.25.

    Step 3: The fee as a yearly interest rate

    An overdraft is really a short loan from the bank. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau found in 2014 that most debit card overdrafts were for $24 or less and were paid back within 3 days, with a typical fee of $34. To compare that with a loan, divide the fee by the amount borrowed, then multiply by how many of those 3-day stretches fit in a year.

    APR = feeamount overdrawn × 365days until repaid
    In this example
    2014, $34 fee
    $34$24 × 3653 = 1.417 × 121.7 = 172.4, which is about 17,000% a year.
    2025, $27 fee
    $27$24 × 3653 = 1.125 × 121.7 = 136.9, which is about 13,700% a year.

    Step 4: A year of fees

    Multiply each fee by how often it happens, then by 12 months.

    Yearly fees = 12 × (times a month × fee)
    In this example
    One overdraft a month
    12 × $26.77 = $321.24 a year.
    Two outside ATMs a month
    12 × 2 × $4.86 = $116.64 a year.

    Worked example: one bad Wednesday

    1. Start. $50 in checking, opted in, charges posted in time order.
    2. Coffee and lunch. $50 − $5.25 − $12.50 = $32.25. Still fine.
    3. Phone bill. $32.25 − $45.00 = −$12.75. The bank pays it and charges $27: now −$39.75.
    4. Gas and streaming. Each one overdraws again: $20.00 + $27 and $11.99 + $27. You end at −$125.74.
    5. Add it up. 3 × $27 = $81 in fees on $94.74 of spending.
    6. The fix. A $100 cushion means $0 in fees. Opting out alone would decline the gas and save $27. Moving the phone bill to Friday, after payday, would save all $81.
    05 Nature link

    Hummingbirds never run the tank to zero.

    A hummingbird's heart can beat more than 1,000 times a minute, and it has to feed every few minutes all day. At night it can't eat. So on cold nights it drops into torpor: its heart slows to as few as 50 beats a minute and its body cools, cutting the energy it burns by as much as 95%. That way it wakes up with fuel left.

    What you're looking at: an illustration, not data. A hummingbird feeds by day, then saves energy in torpor at night.

    Why this sticks. A hummingbird can't let its fuel hit zero overnight, because there's no one to cover the gap. Your account is gentler, but hitting zero still costs $27 a time. The fix is the same: notice when reserves run low, slow your spending way down, and wait for payday to refill the tank.

    09 Check

    Exam-style questions.

    Written the way a college exam would ask them. Each right answer on the first try is worth 10 xp.

    On the job: you're the member service rep

    You work at Riverbend. A member, Marcus, 19, is upset: he was charged $108 in overdraft fees in one day. He started the day with $50, is opted in, and his charges were coffee $5.25, lunch $12.50, phone bill $45.00, gas $20.00 and streaming $11.99. His old bank posted them largest first.

    Your task: explain how $50 turned into $108 of fees, show what time-order posting would have charged, and give him three settings so it never happens again.

    See a model answer

    What happened: largest first, the $45 phone bill posted first and left $5. Then gas ($20), lunch ($12.50), streaming ($11.99) and coffee ($5.25) each overdrew the account: 4 × $27 = $108.

    Time order instead: coffee and lunch clear ($32.25 left), and only the phone bill, gas and streaming overdraw: 3 × $27 = $81. Riverbend posts in time order.

    Three settings: (1) opt out of overdraft coverage, so short card purchases like the gas are declined free; (2) link a Riverbend savings account as overdraft protection and set a low-balance alert at $100; (3) ask the phone company to move his due date to just after payday, which would have avoided every fee that day.

    Also: many banks will refund a first overdraft fee if you ask politely. It's worth asking.
