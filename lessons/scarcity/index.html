Unit 1 · Lesson 1 Scarcity and trade-offs

Every choice has a price tag.

Where the story starts You're 17. It's the summer before senior year, you have $300 saved and about 10 free hours every weekend. You can mow lawns, fix up old bikes to resell, study for the SAT or hang out with friends. You can't do all of it.

What

Decide how to use something limited, like time or money, when you want more than it can cover.

Why

Every yes is a no to something else. Seeing the hidden cost makes your choices sharper.

How

Price each option by its opportunity cost: the best thing you gave up to get it.

Who

Economists study it. Strategy analysts and consultants get paid to make these calls for companies.

    04 Break it down your style

    The logic, step by step.

    Opportunity cost

    Opportunity cost of a choice = value of the best option you gave up

    Only the next best option counts, not all of them added together. If you could have mowed ($15) or babysat ($12), an hour with friends costs $15, not $27.

    Opportunity cost along a frontier

    Cost of 1 more A = B given up ÷ A gained

    Worked example: bikes or lawns

    1. Know your rates. In 10 hours you can mow 10 lawns ($15 each) or fix 2.5 bikes (4 hours each, about $70 profit each).
    2. Price one bike in lawns. One bike takes 4 hours, which could have been 4 lawns. So 1 bike costs 4 lawns, or $60 of mowing.
    3. Compare to what it pays. A bike earns $70. Its opportunity cost is $60. The bike wins by $10.
    4. Watch for rising cost. Your first bike uses your best project. The third one might be a rusty mess that takes 6 hours. Now it costs 6 lawns ($90) and loses. That's why the frontier curves.
    5. Decide at the margin. Don't ask "bikes or lawns?" Ask "is one more bike worth more than what it pushes out?" Stop when the answer flips.
    Go deeper: sunk costs, the bowed frontier and comparative advantage

    Sunk costs don't count. Money or time already spent and unrecoverable shouldn't change your next choice. If you spent 3 hours on a bike that turns out to need $80 in parts, the 3 hours are gone either way. Only compare what's still ahead.

    Why the frontier bows outward. Resources aren't equally good at everything. Shift hours from mowing to bikes and you use your best bike skills first, then worse ones, so each extra bike costs more lawns. Economists call this the law of increasing opportunity cost. On an exam, a straight-line frontier means constant opportunity cost.

    Comparative advantage. Say your friend Sam mows 2 lawns an hour but fixes a bike in 8 hours. Sam's bike costs 16 lawns, yours costs 4. Even if Sam were faster at both, you should fix bikes and Sam should mow, then trade. This idea, from David Ricardo in 1817, is the basis for why countries trade, and it shows up in every Principles of Microeconomics course.

    05 Nature link your style

    A tree runs on a budget.

    A tree makes only so much sugar from sunlight each year. Every bit spent on roots can't go into leaves, and leaves can't become seeds. Trees in dry ground spend on deep roots. Trees in a crowded forest race upward for light. Neither can max out everything.

    The lesson from biology. Every living thing faces scarcity, so every living thing makes trade-offs. Ecologists even call it an "energy budget." The tree doesn't feel bad about not growing seeds in a drought year. It puts its sugar where it matters most right now. That's exactly what you're learning to do with money.

    06 Notes your style

    The page you'd copy down.

    • Scarcity: wants are unlimited, resources (time, money, energy) are limited. It's why economics exists.
    • Trade-off: getting more of one thing means getting less of another.
    • Opportunity cost: the value of the next best alternative you give up. Only the best one counts.
    • Production possibilities frontier (PPF): the most you can make of two things with what you have. On it = efficient. Inside = waste. Outside = impossible for now.
    • Growth (better tools, skills, more time) shifts the frontier outward.
    • Marginal thinking: compare the cost and benefit of one more unit, not all or nothing.
    • Sunk costs are already gone. Ignore them when deciding what's next.

    Fill in the blanks

    1. You skip a $20 shift to see a $12 movie. The opportunity cost of the movie night is the movie ticket plus dollars of lost pay.
    2. A point inside the frontier means resources are being .
    3. If 1 bike takes the same time as 4 lawns, the opportunity cost of 1 bike is lawns.
    4. Money already spent that you can't get back is a cost.
    07 Together your style

    Grab a friend or a study group.

    The $1,000 city council

    Your town has $1,000 left this year. Options: fix a pothole-filled road, add a bus stop, or repaint the skate park. Each person argues for one. Before you vote, everyone has to name the opportunity cost of their own pick.

    Trade like Ricardo

    Time each other: how many words can you type in a minute, and how many math problems can you solve? Work out who has the comparative advantage in each, then split a pretend homework load to finish fastest.

    Hours showdown

    Both play Ten Free Hours and compare your weekends. Did one of you chase money and the other chase SAT hours? Explain the one hour you'd move if you played again.

    09 Check

    Exam-style questions.

    Written the way a college exam would ask them. Each right answer on the first try is worth 10 xp.

    On the job: you're the strategy analyst

    A coffee shop has 40 staff hours a week to spare. It can use them to run a catering service (earns about $1,200 a week in profit) or stay open two extra evenings (earns about $900). A third idea, weekend pop-ups, would earn about $1,000.

    Your task: recommend one option, and state its opportunity cost in dollars. What one fact would make you change your answer?

    See a model answer

    Pick catering. It earns the most profit for the same 40 hours.

    Opportunity cost: $1,000, the profit from pop-ups, the next best option. Not $1,900: you can only give up one alternative with the same hours.

    What would change it: if catering needs $400 a week in van rental that isn't in the estimate, it drops to $800 and pop-ups win. Always check that every option's numbers include all of its costs.
