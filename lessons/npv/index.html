Unit 13 · Lesson 2 Corporate finance

$10,000 now for $15,000 later. Is that a good deal?

The story so far Both shops are open. Now you want a repair van, so you can fix bikes at people's homes. The van costs $10,000 today. You expect it to bring in $3,000 of extra cash a year for 5 years, then it's worn out. That's $15,000 back for $10,000 spent. Sounds like an easy yes. But the $15,000 comes slowly, over five years, and money later is worth less than money now (Lesson 10.2). This lesson shows the tool every company uses to decide what to build: net present value.

What

Net present value (NPV) is what a project's future cash is worth today, minus what it costs today.

Why

Every company has more ideas than money. NPV tells you which ideas make the business more valuable and which ones quietly lose money.

How

List the cash the project brings each year, shrink each amount back to today's dollars, add them up, and subtract the cost.

Who

Corporate finance analysts run NPV on new stores, machines and products. CFOs use it to pick which projects get money. Investment bankers use the same math to value whole companies.

What you're looking at: the van as a timeline. The orange bar is the $10,000 you pay today. The green coins are the $3,000 it brings each year. The dashed rings show $3,000; the solid coins show what it's worth in today's dollars, which shrinks the further away it is.

01 See it

Bring every dollar back to today.

You can't compare $10,000 today with $3,000 in year 5 directly. They're different kinds of dollars. So you turn every future dollar into today's dollars first.

Project cash flows

A project's cash flows are the real money it brings in or costs each year. Use cash, not profit, for the same reason as in Lesson 12.3: you can only spend cash.

In this example
Today
−$10,000 for the van.
Years 1 to 5
+$3,000 each year: $3,000 × 5 = $15,000 in total, before thinking about time.

The discount rate

To shrink future cash into today's dollars, you need a discount rate: the return your money has to earn to be worth tying up. Companies also call it the hurdle rate, because a project has to jump over it. Lesson 13.3 shows how companies set it.

In this example
Your rate
10% a year. Between the bank loan at 8% and what your investor expects, you need the van to earn at least 10%.

Present value of each year

Divide each year's cash by 1.10 once for every year you wait. That's the present value from Lesson 10.2.

Present value = cash that year(1 + rate)year
In this example
Year 1
$3,0001.10 = $2,727
Year 2
$3,0001.21 = $2,479
Years 3, 4, 5
$2,254, $2,049 and $1,863. The later the $3,000, the less it's worth today.

Net present value

Add up every year's present value and subtract the cost. That's the net present value, or NPV. The NPV rule: if NPV is above zero, do the project. It earns more than your discount rate, and the business becomes worth that much more. Below zero, skip it.

NPV = present value of all future cash − cost today
In this example
Add them up
$2,727 + $2,479 + $2,254 + $2,049 + $1,863 = $11,372.
NPV
$11,372 − $10,000 = $1,372. Above zero, so buy the van. The $15,000 is really worth $11,372 today, and that still beats the $10,000 price.

Internal rate of return

Raise the discount rate and NPV falls. At some rate, NPV hits exactly zero. That rate is the internal rate of return, or IRR: the yearly return the project really earns. If the IRR beats your discount rate, the NPV is above zero. Slide the rate below and find it.

In this example
The van's IRR
About 15.2%. At 15%, NPV is +$56. At 16%, it's −$177. Since 15.2% beats your 10%, the van is worth it.

What you're looking at: the van's five years of cash. Each dashed outline is the $3,000 that arrives; the green bar inside is what it's worth today at the rate you pick. The total at the bottom compares all five green bars with the $10,000 cost.

The payback period

Many small businesses ask a simpler question: how long until I get my money back? That's the payback period. It's easy, but it has two blind spots: it treats a dollar in year 3 like a dollar today, and it ignores everything after the money comes back.

Payback = costcash per year   (when the cash is the same every year)
In this example
The van
$10,000$3,000 = about 3.3 years. It says nothing about years 4 and 5.

    Worked example: you can only afford one

    You have $10,000 and two ideas. Here are the facts:

    • The van: costs $10,000 and brings $3,000 a year for 5 years.
    • A parts booth at the market: costs $10,000 and brings $6,600 in year 1, $4,235 in year 2 and $1,331 in year 3.
    • Your rate: 10%.
    1. Payback. The booth gets your money back in about 1.8 years ($6,600 + $4,235 = $10,835 by year 2). The van takes about 3.3 years. Payback says: booth.
    2. The booth's present values. $6,6001.10 = $6,000, $4,2351.21 = $3,500, $1,3311.331 = $1,000.
    3. The booth's NPV. $6,000 + $3,500 + $1,000 − $10,000 = $500.
    4. Compare. The van's NPV is $1,372; the booth's is $500.
    5. The answer. Buy the van. When you can only do one, pick the higher NPV. The booth pays back faster, but the van adds $872 more value, because payback ignores the van's years 4 and 5.
    05 Nature link

    Bees pay up front for wax.

    Honeybees build their comb from wax they make inside their bodies. It's expensive: a colony eats about 6 to 8 pounds of honey to make 1 pound of wax. But a good comb stores honey and raises young bees for years. So colonies build most of their comb when flowers are full of nectar, when the payoff is biggest.

    What you're looking at: an illustration, not data. On the left, the honey a colony spends today. On the right, the comb it builds with that honey, which pays the colony back for years.

    Why this sticks. The colony pays a big cost now for a stream of benefits later, and builds most when the future payoff is largest. That's an NPV decision.

    09 Check

    Exam-style questions.

    Written the way a college exam would ask them. Each right answer on the first try is worth 10 xp.

    On the job: you're a corporate finance analyst

    A coffee chain is thinking about a new espresso machine for its busiest store. Here's the situation:

    • Cost today: $50,000.
    • Extra cash: $12,000 a year from faster service, for 6 years.
    • After 6 years: the machine is worth nothing.
    • The company's discount rate: 8%.

    Your task: what's the payback period? What's the NPV at 8%? Should the company buy it? What if the CFO says the rate should be 12%?

    See a model answer

    Payback: $50,000$12,000 = about 4.2 years.

    NPV at 8%: bringing each $12,000 back to today gives $11,111, $10,288, $9,526, $8,820, $8,167 and $7,562, which add up to about $55,475 (each year is rounded). NPV = $55,475 − $50,000 = $5,475. Above zero, so buy it.

    At 12%: the same six years are worth only about $49,337 today, so NPV is about −$663. Now it's a skip. The decision depends on the rate, which is why the next lesson is about setting it.
