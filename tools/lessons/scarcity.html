Every choice has a price tag.

Where the story starts You're 17, and it's the summer before senior year. You have $300 saved and about 10 free hours every weekend. You could mow lawns for neighbors (about 1 hour and $15 per lawn), fix up old bikes and resell them (about 4 hours and $70 profit per bike), study for the SAT, or hang out with friends. You can't do all of it, so you have to choose.

What

Choosing how to use something you only have a limited amount of, like time or money.

Why

Saying yes to one thing always means saying no to something else. When you can see what you're giving up, you make better choices.

How

Compare each option by its opportunity cost: the best thing you give up to get it.

Who

Economists study these choices. Strategy analysts and consultants get paid to make them for companies.

The green curve below shows the most of both you can finish in one weekend. It's called a production possibilities frontier, or PPF for short.

    The logic, step by step.

    Opportunity cost

    Opportunity cost of a choice = value of the best option you gave up

    You only count the single best option you gave up, not every option added together. Say one hour of your time could earn $15 mowing a lawn or $12 babysitting. If you spend that hour with friends instead, the hour cost you $15, the better of the two. It didn't cost $27, because you could never have mowed and babysat in the same hour.

    Measuring cost in the other thing

    Opportunity cost doesn't have to be in dollars. Often it's easier to ask: "To get one more of this, how much of that do I give up?"

    A bike takes 4 hours. In those same 4 hours you could mow 4 lawns. So one bike costs you 4 lawns. As a formula:

    Cost of 1 more bike = lawns you give upbikes you gain = 41 = 4 lawns

    Worked example: bikes or lawns

    1. Write down what each job takes. A lawn takes 1 hour and pays $15. A bike takes 4 hours and makes about $70 profit.
    2. Find what one bike costs in lawns. The 4 hours a bike takes could have mowed 4 lawns. So one bike costs 4 lawns, which is 4 × $15 = $60 of mowing money.
    3. Compare that cost to what the bike pays. The bike makes $70, and you gave up $60 to fix it. So the bike is the better use of those hours, by $10.
    4. Watch the cost go up. Your first bike is probably an easy one. Your third might be a rusty mess that takes 6 hours. That bike costs 6 lawns ($90) but still only pays $70, so it's no longer worth it. This rising cost is why the frontier is a curve instead of a straight line.
    5. Decide one at a time. Don't ask "bikes or lawns?" Ask "is one more bike worth more than the lawns it replaces?" Keep going until the answer becomes no. Economists call this thinking at the margin.

    Why the frontier bends outward

    On an exam you'll be asked why a PPF is "bowed outward": it bulges away from the corner instead of being a straight line. The short answer is that not everyone (or everything) is equally good at both jobs. Here's what that means with real numbers.

    Say your business grows and two friends work the weekend with you. You'll call the team's effort "resources". Each person can spend the weekend on bikes or on lawns:

    1. Start with everyone mowing. 5 + 6 + 8 = 19 lawns, 0 bikes.
    2. You want some bikes, so who switches first? Maya, because she gives up the fewest lawns per bike. You gain 4 bikes and lose only 5 lawns: 14 lawns, 4 bikes. Cheap bikes.
    3. Want more bikes? Leo switches next. You gain 2 bikes but lose 6 lawns: 8 lawns, 6 bikes. Each bike now costs 3 lawns.
    4. Want every possible bike? Jordan has to switch. You gain just 1 bike and lose 8 lawns: 0 lawns, 7 bikes. That last bike cost 8 lawns.

    Each extra bike costs more lawns than the one before, because you used up the people who are good at bikes first. Plot those four points and you get a curve that bulges outward:

    If all three of you were equally good at both jobs, every bike would cost the same number of lawns, and the PPF would be the straight dashed line. The bulge comes from people (and machines, land and time) being better at some jobs than others. Economists call this the law of increasing opportunity cost.

    Moving on the curve vs. moving the curve

    Exams love this one. Three different things can happen on a PPF, and only one of them moves the curve itself. Remember: the curve shows the most you could make with what you have. So the curve only moves when "the most you could make" changes.

    1. Sliding along the curve: you change your mind, not your ability. You decide to do more bikes and fewer lawns. You're picking a different spot on the same curve, and the most you could make hasn't changed. For a country, "people buy more cars and less wheat" is this case.
    2. Moving from inside up to the curve: you stop wasting. Say Jordan spent the weekend on the couch. Your team was inside the curve. When Jordan gets to work, you move up toward the curve. But the curve stays put, because Jordan could have worked all along. For a country, unemployment falling is this case: people who were idle start working.
    3. The whole curve moves out: you can now make more than was possible before. This only happens when you get more or better resources. You buy a repair stand so bikes go faster (that's the button in the chart above), you learn a new skill, or a fourth friend joins. For a country, it's new technology, more workers, more machines or better education. Economists call this economic growth.

    What doesn't move it: prices. If bikes sell for less, you can still fix exactly as many in a weekend. The PPF counts how much you can make, not what it's worth.

    Go deeper: sunk costs, the bend when you work alone, and comparative advantage

    1. Sunk costs. A sunk cost is time or money you've already spent and can't get back. It shouldn't change what you do next. Say you've put 3 hours into a bike, and then you find out it needs $80 of parts. Those 3 hours are gone whether you finish the bike or not. The only question now is whether finishing it will make more than the $80 and the hours still ahead. If it won't, stop, even though it feels like a waste.

    2. Same idea, one person. The bend also happens when you work alone. Your first bike of the weekend is an easy one. If you keep going, you get to the rusty bikes that take 6 hours each, so each extra bike costs more lawns. Different reasons, same result: the more of one thing you make, the more each extra one costs. Exam tip: if a PPF is a straight line, every unit costs the same amount.

    3. Comparative advantage. Having a comparative advantage means you can do something while giving up less than someone else would. Say your friend Sam can mow 2 lawns an hour, but takes 8 hours to fix a bike. In those 8 hours Sam could have mowed 16 lawns, so a bike costs Sam 16 lawns. A bike only costs you 4. So you should fix the bikes, Sam should mow the lawns, and you split the work. You both end up with more than if each of you did both jobs. This works even when one person is faster at everything. An economist named David Ricardo explained it in 1817, and it's the main reason countries trade with each other. It's on every intro economics exam.

    A tree runs on a budget.

    A tree makes only so much sugar from sunlight each year. Every bit spent on roots can't go into leaves, and leaves can't become seeds. Trees in dry ground spend on deep roots. Trees in a crowded forest race upward for light. Neither can max out everything.

    The lesson from biology. Every living thing faces scarcity, so every living thing makes trade-offs. Ecologists even call it an "energy budget." The tree doesn't feel bad about not growing seeds in a drought year. It puts its sugar where it matters most right now. That's exactly what you're learning to do with money.

    The page you'd copy down.

    • Scarcity: you want more than you have time, money or energy for. That's the reason economics exists.
    • Trade-off: getting more of one thing means getting less of another.
    • Opportunity cost: the value of the next best alternative you give up. Only the best one counts.
    • Production possibilities frontier (PPF): a curve showing the most you can make of two things with what you have. On the curve, nothing is wasted. Inside it, something is wasted. Outside it is impossible for now.
    • Growth (better tools, better skills, more time) moves the frontier outward.
    • Marginal thinking: compare the cost and benefit of one more unit, not all or nothing.
    • Sunk costs are already gone. Ignore them when deciding what's next.

    The $1,000 city council

    Your town has $1,000 left this year. Options: fix a pothole-filled road, add a bus stop, or repaint the skate park. Each person argues for one. Before you vote, everyone has to name the opportunity cost of their own pick.

    Trade like Ricardo

    Time each other for one minute: how many words can each of you type, and how many math problems can each of you solve? Work out who gives up less to do each task (that's comparative advantage), then split a pretend homework load so you finish fastest.

    On the job: you're the strategy analyst

    A coffee shop has 40 staff hours a week to spare. It can use them to run a catering service (earns about $1,200 a week in profit) or stay open two extra evenings (earns about $900). A third idea, weekend pop-ups, would earn about $1,000.

    Your task: recommend one option, and state its opportunity cost in dollars. What one fact would make you change your answer?

    See a model answer

    Pick catering. It earns the most profit for the same 40 hours.

    Opportunity cost: $1,000, the profit from pop-ups, the next best option. Not $1,900: you can only give up one alternative with the same hours.

    What would change it: if catering needs $400 a week in van rental that isn't in the estimate, it drops to $800 and pop-ups win. Always check that every option's numbers include all of its costs.
