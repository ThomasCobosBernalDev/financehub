Your DCF says $660,945. What are shops like yours actually selling for?

The story so far In Lesson 15.1, your DCF said your bike shops are worth about $660,945, and the chain offered $500,000. The chain's buyer pushes back: "A DCF is just your guesses." So you look up what similar bike businesses really sold for. Three nearby shops sold in the last two years, at 5, 6 and 7 times their yearly operating income. Your shops make $110,000 of that profit. This lesson turns those three sales into a price for yours.

What

Comparables, or comps, price a company by looking at what investors pay for similar companies, using ratios called multiples.

Why

It's fast, it's based on real prices, and it's the first number most bankers, buyers and investors check. Stock prices on the news are often quoted as a multiple.

How

Find similar companies, divide each one's price by its profit to get a multiple, pick a typical multiple, and multiply by your company's profit.

Who

Investment banking analysts build "comps tables" for every deal. Equity research analysts compare a stock's multiple with its rivals. Business brokers price small businesses this way.

What you're looking at: three bike shops that sold recently, each tagged with its multiple: how many times its yearly operating income the buyer paid. Your shops have a question mark. Use the middle multiple, 6, and your $110,000 profit: about $660,000, almost exactly what your DCF said.

A multiple is a price per dollar of profit.

A DCF builds the value from your own forecast. Comps start from the other end: what are real buyers paying right now for businesses like yours? Bankers almost always do both and compare.

Multiples

You can't compare prices directly; a bigger shop costs more. So you divide each price by a measure of how much the business earns. That ratio is a multiple: how many dollars a buyer pays for each dollar of profit.

Multiple = priceprofit
In this example
The first shop that sold
Price $500,000, yearly profit $100,000: $500,000$100,000 = 5x, read "5 times."

The P/E ratio

The multiple you'll hear most for stocks is the P/E ratio: the share price divided by earnings per share (EPS), the company's net profit split across its shares.

P/E = share priceearnings per share
In this example
The bike maker from Lesson 14.3
Its stock is $30 and it earned $2 per share: $30$2 = a P/E of 15. Investors pay $15 for each $1 of yearly profit.
Going the other way
If a rival earns $3 a share and similar stocks trade at a P/E of 15, its stock should be about 15 × $3 = $45.

EBITDA and EV/EBITDA

For buying a whole business, bankers use EBITDA (say "ee-bit-dah"): operating income (EBIT, Lesson 12.1) with depreciation added back. It's a rough measure of the cash the business itself makes, before lenders and taxes. They divide enterprise value (Lesson 15.1) by it to get EV/EBITDA.

EBITDA = operating income + depreciation
EV/EBITDA = enterprise valueEBITDA
In this example
Your shops' EBITDA
Operating profit $95,000 + depreciation on tools and the van $15,000 = $110,000.

Match the top and the bottom. EBITDA comes before interest, so it belongs to lenders and owners together; pair it with enterprise value, which is also for both. Earnings per share come after interest, so they belong only to owners; pair them with the share price.

Pick good comps, then the middle one

Comparable companies should be in the same industry, with similar size, growth and risk. Prices paid to buy whole similar companies are called precedent transactions. Then use the median, the middle number once sorted, so one strange sale can't pull the answer too far.

In this example
Shop A
Sold for $500,000; EBITDA $100,000: $500,000$100,000 = 5x.
Shop B
Sold for $480,000; EBITDA $80,000: $480,000$80,000 = 6x.
Shop C
Sold for $840,000; EBITDA $120,000: $840,000$120,000 = 7x.
Left out
An electric bike startup sold at 15x. It's growing fast and isn't like your shops, so it's not a comp.
Median
5x, 6x, 7x: the middle is 6x.

Apply it

Multiply the multiple by your own number. Then, just like in Lesson 15.1, subtract debt and add cash to get the owners' share.

Enterprise value = multiple × your EBITDA
In this example
Your shops
6 × $110,000 = $660,000. Your DCF said $660,945. Two methods, nearly the same answer.
Owners' share
$660,000 − $40,000 loan + $20,000 cash = $640,000.
The chain's offer
$500,000$110,000 = about 4.5x, below every shop that sold.

Why multiples differ

A higher multiple means investors pay more today for each dollar of profit. They do that when they expect that profit to grow faster, or when it's less risky. That's why a fast-growing software company can trade at a P/E of 40 while a steady utility trades at 15.

The weak spot: comps tell you what others are paying, not what a business is truly worth. If the whole market is overpriced, the comps are too. In the 1999 to 2000 dot-com bubble, many internet stocks looked "cheap" next to other internet stocks, and then most of them crashed.

Put it on a football field

Bankers show every method's range on one chart called a football field, because the bars look like a field's yard lines. Where the bars overlap is the fair price. Slide the multiple to move your comps value.

    Worked example: answering the chain

    The chain's buyer says, "Your DCF is just guesses." Here are the facts:

    • Your DCF: $660,945 at 10%, from about $523,441 at 12% to $890,765 at 8%.
    • Comps: 5x to 7x your $110,000 EBITDA, so $550,000 to $770,000.
    • The offer: $500,000.
    1. Find the overlap. The DCF range ($523,441 to $890,765) and the comps range ($550,000 to $770,000) overlap from $550,000 to $770,000.
    2. Check the offer. $500,000 is below both ranges. It's about 4.5x EBITDA, lower than any shop that sold.
    3. Pick your ask. Both middles land near $660,000: 6 × $110,000 = $660,000, and the DCF at 10% gives $660,945.
    4. Expect pushback. The buyer may argue your shops deserve a lower multiple, maybe because they're smaller than Shop C. Be ready with your growth and how steady your profit is.
    5. The answer. Counter at about $660,000 and show the football field. Two methods agreeing makes your number much harder to argue with.

    On the job: you're an investment banking analyst

    A client wants to sell their chain of gyms. Your managing director wants a comps value by tomorrow. Here's the situation:

    • The gyms' EBITDA: $8 million.
    • Five recent gym sales, EV/EBITDA: 7x, 8x, 9x, 10x and 14x. The 14x was a fast-growing luxury chain.
    • Debt: $12 million. Cash: $2 million.
    • The owner hopes for: $100 million for the whole business.

    Your task: find the median multiple and the enterprise value. What range would you show? What's the owners' share? What multiple would $100 million take, and what would you tell the owner?

    See a model answer

    Median: sorted, 7x, 8x, 9x, 10x, 14x; the middle is 9x. Enterprise value: 9 × $8 million = $72 million.

    Range: the luxury chain isn't a good comp, so use 8x to 10x: $64 million to $80 million.

    Owners' share: $72 million − $12 million + $2 million = $62 million.

    The owner's hope: $100 million$8 million = 12.5x, above every typical sale. Only a fast-growing chain got more. Unless the client can show growth like that, we should set expectations around $64 to $80 million.
