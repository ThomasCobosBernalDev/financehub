Last lesson you guessed 10%. Now find the real number.

The story so far In Lesson 13.2 you judged the van with a 10% discount rate. But where should that number come from? Your shop's money comes from two places: a $40,000 bank loan at 8%, and $60,000 of owners' money, yours and your investor's. Neither one is free. The bank wants its interest, and the owners want a return big enough to be worth the risk. Blend the two, and you get the shop's real hurdle: the cost of capital.

What

The cost of capital is the average yearly return a company must pay its lenders and owners for using their money.

Why

It's the hurdle every project has to clear. A project that earns less than it destroys value, even if it makes a profit.

How

Find what debt costs after taxes, find what owners expect, then average the two by how much of each the company uses.

Who

Corporate treasurers manage the mix of debt and equity. Equity research analysts and investment bankers estimate it to value companies. CFOs set it as the hurdle rate.

Every dollar has a price.

In Lesson 13.1 you learned the two kinds of money. Now you put a price on each, and blend them into one number.

Cost of debt, after taxes

The cost of debt starts with the interest rate. But interest is an expense on the income statement (Lesson 12.1), so it lowers taxes. That makes debt cheaper than its rate. To keep the math simple, this lesson uses a flat 25% tax rate.

After-tax cost of debt = interest rate × (1 − tax rate)
In this example
The bank loan
8% × (1 − 0.25) = 8% × 0.75 = 6%. Every $100 of interest saves $25 of tax.

Cost of equity

The cost of equity is the return owners expect. Nobody sends a bill for it, but if owners don't earn it, they sell their shares or stop investing. It's always higher than the cost of debt, because owners get paid last: lenders get their interest first, even in a bad year (Lesson 13.1).

In this example
Your owners
Expect 15% a year. The bank only asks 8%, because it gets paid first.

The CAPM: where 15% comes from

Wall Street estimates the cost of equity with the CAPM, the capital asset pricing model. It starts from the risk-free rate, what U.S. government bonds pay. Then it adds extra return for risk: the market risk premium, times the company's beta, which measures how much it swings compared with the market.

Cost of equity = risk-free rate + beta × market risk premium
In this example
Risk-free rate
4% from government bonds.
Beta
2. A small, young shop swings about twice as much as the market.
Market risk premium
5.5%.
Cost of equity
4% + 2 × 5.5% = 4% + 11% = 15%.

Weights

A company's mix of debt and equity is its capital structure. Each kind of money counts by its share of the total.

Debt weight = debtdebt + equity
In this example
Debt
$40,000$100,000 = 40%
Equity
$60,000$100,000 = 60%

WACC: the blend

Multiply each cost by its weight and add them up. That's the weighted average cost of capital, or WACC, the company's cost of capital.

WACC = debt weight × after-tax cost of debt + equity weight × cost of equity
In this example
Debt part
0.40 × 6% = 2.4%
Equity part
0.60 × 15% = 9%
WACC
2.4% + 9% = 11.4%

WACC is the hurdle

WACC is the discount rate to use for projects as risky as the business itself. A project is worth doing when its IRR beats the WACC, which is the same as having an NPV above zero at the WACC.

In this example
The van
IRR 15.2% beats 11.4%. At 11.4%, its NPV is about $977, less than the $1,372 you found at 10%, but still above zero. Buy it.
The parts booth
IRR about 13.6% also beats 11.4%. NPV about $300.

    Worked example: is more debt always cheaper?

    The slider makes it look like piling on debt keeps lowering WACC. Here's what really happens if the shop borrows until debt is 70% of its money:

    • The bank: sees a riskier borrower and charges 12% instead of 8%.
    • The owners: now get paid after a much bigger interest bill, so they want 20% instead of 15%.
    • Tax rate: still 25%.
    1. After-tax cost of debt. 12% × 0.75 = 9%.
    2. Debt part. 0.70 × 9% = 6.3%.
    3. Equity part. 0.30 × 20% = 6%.
    4. WACC. 6.3% + 6% = 12.3%, higher than the 11.4% you started with.
    5. The answer. No. A little debt is cheap, but too much makes everyone demand more. Companies look for a middle mix where WACC is lowest.

    Crows only crack the big shells.

    On the Pacific coast of Canada, crows eat sea snails called whelks. To crack one, a crow flies straight up about 5 meters and drops it on the rocks, again and again until it breaks. Every flight costs energy. Scientists found that crows pick only the largest whelks: small ones take just as many drops and hold too little food to pay for the flights.

    What you're looking at: an illustration, not data. A crow carrying a whelk up to drop it on the rocks. On the left, the big whelks crows choose; on the right, the small ones they leave.

    Why this sticks. Every flight has a cost, so a whelk has to return more food than the flights take. A project has to return more than the cost of the money behind it. Both are hurdles.

    On the job: you're an equity research analyst

    You're estimating the cost of capital for a grocery chain. Here's the situation:

    • Equity: $600 million (what all its shares are worth on the stock market).
    • Debt: $400 million, at 6% interest.
    • Tax rate: 21%, the U.S. federal rate for corporations.
    • Risk-free rate: 4%. Beta: 1.2. Market risk premium: 5%.
    • A new project: a delivery service with an expected IRR of 9%.

    Your task: find the after-tax cost of debt, the cost of equity, the weights and the WACC. Should the chain launch the delivery service?

    See a model answer

    After-tax cost of debt: 6% × (1 − 0.21) = 6% × 0.79 = 4.74%.

    Cost of equity: 4% + 1.2 × 5% = 4% + 6% = 10%.

    Weights: $400 million$1,000 million = 40% debt, $600 million$1,000 million = 60% equity.

    WACC: 0.40 × 4.74% + 0.60 × 10% = 1.896% + 6% = about 7.9%.

    Decision: the delivery service's 9% IRR beats the 7.9% hurdle, so its NPV is above zero. Launch it, as long as it's about as risky as the rest of the business.
