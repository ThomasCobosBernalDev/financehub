$20,000 came in. How much was profit?

The story so far Your tune-up business is now an LLC with a small shop. In its first full year you did 500 tune-ups at $40: $20,000 of sales. It felt like a lot. But there were parts, $800 a month of rent, insurance, ads, a tool kit and a small loan. The bank wants to see how the year went before it lends you more. So you need the one page every business uses to answer "did we make money?": the income statement.

What

An income statement lists what a business earned and what it spent over a period, like a year, and ends with its profit.

Why

Banks, investors and owners all read it first. It shows not just whether a business made money, but where the money went.

How

Start with sales at the top, subtract costs in a fixed order, and read the profit at the bottom.

Who

Accountants prepare it. Bankers and credit analysts use it to decide on loans. Equity research analysts on Wall Street study public companies' income statements every quarter.

What you're looking at: the shape of an income statement. Sales sit at the top, called the top line. Each cost below gets subtracted. What's left at the bottom is net income, the bottom line: $2,800 out of $20,000.

From the top line to the bottom line.

An income statement always goes in the same order, so you can read anyone's, from a lemonade stand to Apple. Each step subtracts one kind of cost.

Revenue

Revenue is everything you earned from sales. It's the first line, so people call it the top line. Like profit in Lesson 11.2, revenue counts a sale when you do the work, even if the customer pays later.

In this example
Revenue
500 tune-ups × $40 = $20,000.

Cost of goods sold and gross profit

Cost of goods sold (COGS) is the direct cost of what you sold, like the variable costs from Lesson 11.1. Subtract it from revenue to get gross profit. Divide gross profit by revenue for the gross margin.

Gross profit = revenue − cost of goods sold
In this example
COGS
Parts: 500 × $10 = $5,000.
Gross profit
$20,000 − $5,000 = $15,000.
Gross margin
$15,000$20,000 = 0.75, so 75%.

Operating expenses

Operating expenses are the costs of running the business that don't go up with each sale, like the fixed costs from Lesson 11.1: rent, insurance, ads and salaries.

In this example
Rent
12 × $800 = $9,600.
Insurance and small tools
$1,200.
Ads
$600.

Depreciation

When you buy something that lasts for years, like a machine or a truck, you don't count the whole price as a cost in the year you buy it. You spread it over the years it'll be used. That yearly slice is depreciation, and it's an operating expense. The cash left all at once; the expense shows up a little each year.

Yearly depreciation = priceyears it will be used
In this example
A $3,000 repair stand and tool kit
It should last 5 years: $3,0005 = $600 a year.
Total operating expenses
$9,600 + $1,200 + $600 + $600 = $12,000.

Operating income

Operating income is what the business itself earns from its main work. Wall Street often calls it EBIT, earnings before interest and taxes. The operating margin is operating income divided by revenue.

Operating income = gross profit − operating expenses
In this example
Operating income
$15,000 − $12,000 = $3,000.
Operating margin
$3,000$20,000 = 0.15, so 15%.

Interest, taxes and net income

Next come the costs of how the business is paid for and taxed. Interest on loans is an expense, but paying back the loan itself, the principal, is not: that's just returning borrowed money. Then income tax: a C corporation pays 21% here, while a pass-through LLC shows $0, because the owner pays on a personal return (Lesson 11.3). What's left is net income, the bottom line. The net margin is net income divided by revenue.

Net income = operating income − interest − taxes
In this example
Interest
On a small equipment loan: $200.
Taxes
Your LLC is pass-through: $0 on this statement.
Net income
$3,000 − $200 − $0 = $2,800.
Net margin
$2,800$20,000 = 0.14, so 14%: 14 cents of every dollar.

    Trees have a gross and a net, too.

    Ecologists measure plants the same way accountants measure businesses. Everything a plant makes from sunlight is its gross production. It burns a big share of that, often about half, just to stay alive. What's left over, the net production, is what becomes new wood, leaves and seeds.

    What you're looking at: an illustration, not data. Sunlight comes into a tree on the left, some energy goes out as the tree's own upkeep at the top, and the rest becomes a new growth ring, shown in green on the right.

    Why this sticks. A big tree in the sun can still grow slowly if its upkeep is high. A business with big revenue can still earn little if its costs eat the top line. Always read down to the net.

    On the job: you're an equity research analyst

    You cover Trailhead Gear, a company that sells hiking gear. It's a C corporation. Here's its year:

    • Revenue: $5,000,000.
    • Cost of goods sold: $3,000,000.
    • Operating expenses: $1,500,000.
    • Interest: $100,000.
    • Tax rate: 21%.

    Your task: work out gross profit, operating income and net income, and the gross and net margins. A rival has a 50% gross margin. What might explain the difference?

    See a model answer

    Gross profit: $5,000,000 − $3,000,000 = $2,000,000. Gross margin: $2,000,000$5,000,000 = 40%.

    Operating income: $2,000,000 − $1,500,000 = $500,000 (10%).

    Net income: $500,000 − $100,000 = $400,000 before tax. Tax: $400,000 × 0.21 = $84,000. Net income: $316,000. Net margin: $316,000$5,000,000 = 6.32%.

    The gap: the rival keeps 50 cents of each sales dollar after direct costs; Trailhead keeps 40. The rival might charge higher prices for a stronger brand, buy its gear more cheaply, or sell more of its own products instead of other brands'.
