The year is over. What's the business worth today?

The story so far Your income statement said the shop earned $2,800 this year. The bank liked that, but it asked a second question: "What does the business have right now, and what does it owe?" On December 31 the shop has $2,600 in the bank, a customer who owes it $1,000, parts on the shelf, and the tool kit. It also owes a parts supplier and still owes $2,000 on its loan. Answering the bank takes the second financial statement: the balance sheet.

What

A balance sheet lists what a business owns, what it owes, and what's left for the owners, on one exact day.

Why

Profit tells you how a year went. The balance sheet tells you how strong the business is right now: can it pay its bills, and how much does it lean on debt?

How

List the assets, list the liabilities, and the difference is equity. The two sides always match.

Who

Accountants and auditors make sure it adds up. Credit analysts and commercial lenders read it before every loan. Private equity and investment bankers dig through it before buying a company.

What you're looking at: the shop's balance sheet as a scale. On the left, everything it owns: $7,000. On the right, who has a claim on those things: lenders and suppliers are owed $3,000, and the other $4,000 belongs to you, the owner. The scale is always level.

Owns, owes, and what's yours.

The income statement is a video of a whole year. The balance sheet is a photo of one day. It has three parts, and they always fit one equation.

Assets

Assets are everything the business owns that's worth something. Current assets turn into cash within a year: cash, accounts receivable from Lesson 11.2, and inventory. Long-term assets, like equipment, last for years.

In this example
Current assets
Cash $2,600 + receivable $1,000 + parts inventory $1,000 = $4,600.
Long-term asset
The tool kit, worth $2,400 on the books (see below).
Total assets
$4,600 + $2,400 = $7,000.

Book value and accumulated depreciation

Remember the $3,000 tool kit and its $600 a year of depreciation from Lesson 12.1? The balance sheet keeps a running total of all the depreciation so far, called accumulated depreciation. Price minus accumulated depreciation is the asset's book value.

Book value = price − accumulated depreciation
In this example
After 1 year
$3,000 − $600 = $2,400.
After 3 years
$3,000 − 3 × $600 = $1,200.

Liabilities

Liabilities are everything the business owes. Current liabilities are due within a year, like accounts payable. Long-term liabilities, like most of a loan, are due later.

In this example
Current
Accounts payable to the parts supplier: $1,000.
Long-term
The equipment loan: $2,000.
Total liabilities
$1,000 + $2,000 = $3,000.

Equity

Equity is what's left for the owners: assets minus liabilities. It has two pieces: the money owners put in, and retained earnings, the profit the business has earned and kept. For a person, the same idea is called net worth.

In this example
Money you put in
$1,200 of your savings, when you opened the shop.
Retained earnings
This year's net income, all kept in the business: $2,800.
Total equity
$1,200 + $2,800 = $4,000.

The accounting equation

Everything a business owns was paid for by someone: lenders or owners. So the two sides always match. That's the accounting equation, and it's why it's called a balance sheet. Every transaction changes at least two things, so it stays balanced.

Assets = liabilities + equity
In this example
Check
$7,000 = $3,000 + $4,000. It balances.

Can it pay its bills?

Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities. The current ratio divides them instead. Lenders like to see a current ratio above 1, often 1.5 or more.

Current ratio = current assetscurrent liabilities
In this example
Working capital
$4,600 − $1,000 = $3,600.
Current ratio
$4,600$1,000 = 4.6. Plenty of cushion.

How much does it lean on debt?

The debt-to-equity ratio compares what the business owes to what the owners have in it. Higher means more borrowed money: bigger returns in good years, bigger danger in bad ones.

Debt-to-equity = total liabilitiesequity
In this example
The shop
$3,000$4,000 = 0.75: 75 cents of debt for every dollar of yours.

    On the job: you're a commercial lender

    A bakery asks your bank for a new $30,000 loan to buy a second oven. Here's its balance sheet today:

    • Current assets: cash $20,000, receivables $5,000, inventory $15,000.
    • Long-term assets: equipment with a book value of $60,000.
    • Current liabilities: $20,000 of bills due this year.
    • Long-term liabilities: a $40,000 loan.
    • Your bank's rule: debt-to-equity must stay at 2 or below.

    Your task: find total assets, total liabilities and equity. Work out the current ratio and debt-to-equity. Does the $30,000 loan fit the bank's rule? If not, what could the bakery do?

    See a model answer

    Totals: assets $20,000 + $5,000 + $15,000 + $60,000 = $100,000. Liabilities $20,000 + $40,000 = $60,000. Equity $100,000 − $60,000 = $40,000.

    Current ratio: $40,000$20,000 = 2.0. Healthy.

    Debt-to-equity today: $60,000$40,000 = 1.5.

    With the new loan: $90,000$40,000 = 2.25, over the limit. The most the bank can lend is $40,000 × 2 − $60,000 = $20,000. The owner could borrow $20,000 and put in $10,000 of their own money, or wait a year and let retained earnings grow equity first.
