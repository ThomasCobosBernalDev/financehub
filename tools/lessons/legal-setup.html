The stand is a real business now. Time to act like one.

The story so far Your tune-up stand made $8,400 of profit this year: $5,400 from regular months plus $3,000 from the bike club. You never filled out a single form, so in the eyes of the law you're a sole proprietor: you and the business are the same person. Now you want to rent a small shop on a 1-year lease at $800 a month. If the business fails, can the landlord take your $3,600 emergency fund? And how much tax do you owe on that $8,400? This lesson is about the choices that turn a side hustle into a company.

What

A business structure is the legal form a business takes. It decides who's on the hook for its debts and how its profit gets taxed.

Why

The wrong setup can cost you your personal savings if things go bad, or thousands in extra tax if things go well.

How

Pick a structure, register it with your state, get a tax ID, open a business bank account, and pay tax four times a year.

Who

Small-business accountants and CPAs help owners pick a structure and file taxes. Business lawyers write the paperwork. At big companies, tax analysts handle the same questions with far bigger numbers.

What you're looking at: the business on the left and you on the right. With an LLC, a legal wall stands between them: if the shop can't pay its lease, the landlord can go after what the business owns, but not your personal savings.

Who's on the hook, and who pays the tax.

Every business structure answers two questions. If the business owes money, can they come after your stuff? And when the business makes a profit, how many times does it get taxed?

Sole proprietorship

If you start selling on your own and file nothing, you're a sole proprietorship. It's free and automatic. But you and the business are legally the same, so you have unlimited liability: the business's debts are your debts.

In this example
The lease
12 months × $800 = $9,600 you promise to pay.
If the shop closes after 3 months
9 × $800 = $7,200 still owed. As a sole proprietor, the landlord can go after your $3,600 emergency fund.

Partnership

Two or more people who go into business together without filing anything form a general partnership. Same deal as a sole proprietor, times two: each partner can be on the hook for the whole debt, even one the other partner signed.

In this example
If a friend joins
You split the profit, but the landlord could still collect the full $7,200 from either of you.

LLC

A limited liability company (LLC) is a business you register with your state, usually for $50 to $500, plus a yearly fee in many states. It gives you limited liability: you can lose what's in the business, but usually not your personal savings. Two catches: if you sign a personal guarantee, you're on the hook anyway, and the wall doesn't cover harm you cause yourself, which is what liability insurance is for.

In this example
The same $7,200
The landlord can take what the LLC owns, like its tools and bank account. Your $3,600 stays yours, as long as you didn't personally guarantee the lease.

Pass-through taxes

Sole proprietorships, partnerships and most small LLCs are pass-through businesses. The business itself pays no income tax. The profit lands on your personal tax return, and you pay income tax plus the 15.3% self-employment tax from Lesson 2.3.

In this example
Self-employment tax
$8,400 × 0.9235 = $7,757.40. × 0.153 = $1,186.88, on top of any income tax.

Corporations and double taxation

A C corporation is its own taxpayer: it pays a 21% federal tax on its profit. When it pays what's left to its owners as dividends, the owners pay tax again, usually 15%. That's double taxation. Why bother? Corporations can sell shares to many investors, and profit kept in the business isn't taxed a second time. Most startups raising money and every company on the stock market are corporations. (An LLC can also choose to be taxed as an S corporation, a pass-through that can cut self-employment tax once profit grows; that's a question for a CPA.)

In this example
If the business made $100,000
Corporate tax: $100,000 × 0.21 = $21,000. Left: $79,000.
Paid out as dividends
$79,000 × 0.15 = $11,850. You keep $67,150. Total tax: $32,850.
The owner's income tax rate

What you're looking at: $100,000 of profit, taxed two ways. The top bar is a pass-through LLC: taxed once, at the owner's rate. The bottom bar is a C corporation: 21%, then a dividend tax on whatever it pays out (0% for owners in the 12% bracket, 15% otherwise). Orange is tax; green is what the owner and the business keep. Simplified: it skips state taxes, self-employment tax and deductions.

The paperwork

Making it official takes a short checklist. Register the LLC with your state. Get an EIN, a free tax ID for the business from the IRS. Get any license your city or county requires. Open a separate business bank account, so business and personal money never mix; mixing them can weaken your LLC's wall. And since nobody withholds tax from business profit, pay estimated taxes every quarter if you expect to owe $1,000 or more.

In this example
Quarterly payments
Self-employment tax alone is $1,186.88, over $1,000, so pay quarterly: $1,186.884 = about $297 each quarter.

    Worked example: is the LLC worth it?

    1. The cost. Say your state charges $100 to file and $50 a year after that.
    2. The risk. If the shop closes after 3 months, $7,200 of lease is still owed.
    3. Without an LLC. Your whole $3,600 emergency fund could go toward it.
    4. With an LLC. The landlord collects from the business, not from you, unless you signed a personal guarantee.
    5. The answer. $100 to protect $3,600 is a good deal, and your taxes don't change: a one-owner LLC is still pass-through.

    On the job: you're a small-business accountant

    Jordan runs a dog-walking business and asks you for help. Here's the situation:

    • Structure: none filed, so a sole proprietorship.
    • Profit this year: $40,000.
    • Taxes paid so far: $0. Nobody withholds anything.
    • Personal savings: $15,000.
    • Next step: Jordan wants to sign a $30,000 loan for a van for the business.

    Your task: about how much self-employment tax does Jordan owe? Should Jordan be paying quarterly? What setup would you recommend before the van loan?

    See a model answer

    Self-employment tax: $40,000 × 0.9235 = $36,940. × 0.153 = $5,651.82, plus income tax.

    Quarterly? Yes. That's well over $1,000, so Jordan should pay estimated taxes every quarter: $5,651.824 = about $1,413 each, just for self-employment tax.

    Setup: form an LLC so a business debt can't reach the $15,000 of savings. Taxes stay the same, because a one-owner LLC is pass-through. Get an EIN, open a business bank account, and buy liability insurance in case a dog gets hurt. Warn Jordan that the lender may ask for a personal guarantee on the van, which would put the savings back on the hook for that loan.
