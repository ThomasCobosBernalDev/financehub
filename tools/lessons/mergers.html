Two buyers want your shops. Why can both pay more than they're worth?

The story so far Your DCF and your comps both say your bike shops are worth about $660,000 (Lessons 15.1 and 15.2). Word gets out, and now two buyers are bidding. The bike chain raises its offer to $700,000; it says it can save $20,000 a year by running your shops with its own. A private equity firm offers $660,000, and says it will borrow most of the money. This lesson shows how each buyer does the math, and why neither is crazy.

What

Mergers and acquisitions (M&A) are deals where one company buys another or two combine. A leveraged buyout (LBO) is a purchase paid for mostly with borrowed money.

Why

Companies buy others to grow faster or cut costs. Private equity firms buy companies to improve them and sell them a few years later at a profit.

How

Start with what the target is worth on its own, add the value of combining, and decide how much to pay with cash, debt or stock.

Who

M&A bankers advise buyers and sellers and run the sale. Private equity associates build LBO models to decide what to bid. Corporate development teams find companies for their employer to buy.

What you're looking at: why a buyer can pay more than a company is worth on its own. Your shops (blue) are worth $660,000 alone. Combined with the chain (green), they save $20,000 a year. At the 6x multiple from Lesson 15.2, those savings are worth 6 × $20,000 = $120,000. That extra is the overlap.

Buying a company is buying its future cash.

Everything from Lessons 15.1 and 15.2 still applies: a company is worth its future cash. A deal adds two questions. What extra value does the buyer create? And how is the buyer paying?

Mergers and acquisitions

In an acquisition, one company, the acquirer, buys another, the target. In a merger, two companies combine into one. Together they're called M&A. Before any deal closes, the buyer does due diligence: a deep check of the target's books, contracts and risks.

In this example
Acquirer
The bike chain (or the private equity firm).
Target
Your two bike shops.
Due diligence
The buyer checks your three financial statements, your $40,000 loan and your shop leases.

The control premium

Owners rarely sell at today's price; why would they? Buyers usually pay more than the company's current value to get control. That extra is the control premium. For public companies it's often 20% to 40% above the share price.

Control premium = offer price − current pricecurrent price
In this example
The chain's offer
$700,000 − $660,000$660,000 = $40,000$660,000 = about 6%.
A public company
A stock trades at $20, and a buyer offers $25 a share: $25 − $20$20 = a 25% premium.

Synergies: why buyers can pay more

How can the chain pay more than your shops are worth? Because together they'll make more than apart. That extra value is synergies: cost savings, like buying parts in bulk or sharing one accounting team, or extra sales. The most a buyer should pay is the target's own value plus the synergies. Pay more, and the buyer loses value.

Most a buyer should pay = target's value on its own + value of synergies
In this example
Synergies
$20,000 a year of savings × the 6x multiple = $120,000.
The chain's most
$660,000 + $120,000 = $780,000. Paying $700,000 still leaves the chain $80,000 ahead.

Promised synergies often don't fully arrive, and many studies find most acquirers end up paying too much. Experienced buyers count only the savings they're sure of.

Two kinds of buyers

A strategic buyer is a company in the same or a related business, like the chain. It can get synergies, so it can often pay more. A financial buyer, usually a private equity (PE) firm, buys companies to improve them and sell them, usually 3 to 7 years later.

The leveraged buyout

PE firms usually pay with mostly borrowed money. That's a leveraged buyout (LBO). The loan sits on the company being bought, and the company's own cash pays it down. When the firm sells, it keeps whatever is left after repaying the debt.

In this example
The PE firm buys
Price $660,000 (6x your $110,000 EBITDA). It borrows 60%, $396,000, and puts in $264,000 of its own money.
Over 5 years
To keep the math simple, say the shops produce $200,000 of cash after interest. All of it pays down the loan: $396,000 − $200,000 = $196,000 left.
The sale
EBITDA grows to $140,000. At the same 6x: 6 × $140,000 = $840,000.
The firm keeps
$840,000 − $196,000 of debt = $644,000.

Measuring the return: MOIC and IRR

PE firms judge a deal two ways. The MOIC (multiple on invested capital, say "moe-ick") is money out divided by money in. The IRR (Lesson 13.2) is the yearly return that turns money in into money out. Two quick rules: doubling your money in 5 years is about 15% a year; tripling it is about 25%.

MOIC = money outmoney in
In this example
With 60% debt
$644,000$264,000 = 2.44x in 5 years, an IRR of about 19.5%.
With no debt
Put in all $660,000, keep the $200,000 of cash, sell for $840,000: $1,040,000$660,000 = 1.58x, an IRR of about 9.5%.

Debt cuts both ways

Debt makes the return bigger because the firm uses less of its own money, but the lenders still only get their fixed amount back. In a bad year, the same debt makes losses bigger, and if the company can't make its payments, the owners can lose everything. Toys "R" Us was bought in a $6.6 billion LBO in 2005; buried in debt, it went bankrupt in 2017 and closed its U.S. stores in 2018. Slide the debt and compare a good case with a bad case.

Bad case

    Worked example: which offer do you take?

    You have two offers on the table. Here are the facts:

    • The chain: $700,000. It can afford up to $780,000 because of synergies.
    • The PE firm: $660,000, with 60% debt. It wants an IRR of 20% or more.
    • Your loan: $40,000. Cash in the bank: $20,000.
    1. Compare prices. The chain offers $700,000 − $660,000 = $40,000 more.
    2. Find the room. The chain could go up to $780,000 and still come out ahead, so there's up to $80,000 more to ask for.
    3. Check the PE firm's limit. At $660,000 its IRR is about 19.5%, just under its 20% goal. It can't raise its bid much.
    4. What you keep. At $700,000, you repay the $40,000 loan and keep the $20,000 cash: $700,000 − $40,000 + $20,000 = $680,000.
    5. The answer. Use the PE bid to push the chain higher. Ask for about $740,000, which splits the synergies with the chain: it still gains $40,000, and you get paid for value your shops help create.

    Lichen: a merger that conquered rock.

    A lichen looks like one living thing, but it's two: a fungus and a tiny alga living as a team. The alga makes food from sunlight; the fungus builds the body, holds water and grips the stone. Neither could survive alone on bare rock or Arctic tundra. Together they cover about 6% of Earth's land.

    What you're looking at: an illustration, not data. A fungus and an alga on their own, then joined as a lichen growing on bare rock, where neither could live alone.

    Why this sticks. A good merger is a lichen: each side brings what the other lacks, and together they can do something neither could alone. That extra is the synergy. A deal with no real synergy is just two companies sharing a rock.

    On the job: you're a private equity associate

    Your firm wants to buy a company that makes pet food. The partner asks if the deal works. Here's the situation:

    • EBITDA today: $10 million. Price: 8x, so $80 million.
    • Paid with: 60% debt ($48 million) and $32 million of the fund's money.
    • In 5 years: EBITDA grows to $13 million, and the company's cash pays the debt down to $28 million.
    • The fund's goal: an IRR of at least 20%.

    Your task: if you sell at the same 8x, what does the fund get back? What's the MOIC? Does it reach about 20%? What if you could sell at 9x? What would you tell the partner?

    See a model answer

    Sale at 8x: 8 × $13 million = $104 million. Minus $28 million of debt: the fund gets $76 million.

    MOIC: $76 million$32 million = about 2.4x, an IRR of about 19%: between doubling (15%) and tripling (25%), just under the goal.

    Sale at 9x: 9 × $13 million = $117 million − $28 million = $89 million. $89 million$32 million = about 2.8x, an IRR of about 23%.

    To the partner: at the same multiple, the deal falls just short of 20%. It only clears the bar if we can sell at a higher multiple or grow EBITDA faster, and we shouldn't count on a higher multiple. I'd try to buy at a lower price, around 7.5x, or find real cost savings before we bid.
